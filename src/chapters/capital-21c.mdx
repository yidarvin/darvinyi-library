## The thesis

Piketty's central claim is a warning about a tendency, not a law of nature. When returns on
capital remain above economic growth, and owners can retain and reinvest a meaningful share
of those returns, accumulated wealth can gain ground on new income. Because ownership begins
unevenly distributed, the result can be a widening gap between those who live chiefly from
work and those who also own claims on land, businesses, housing, and financial assets.
History, policy, and shocks can interrupt the process. They are part of the explanation, not
footnotes to it.

## Why it matters

Inequality is often discussed as a difference in paychecks. This book asks the prior
question: who owns the assets that produce income, and how does that ownership change over
generations? That lens makes inheritance, taxes, debt, housing, corporate ownership, and
public wealth visible in the same frame. It also stops a rising national average from being
mistaken for a broadly shared gain.

## Capital is a stock; income is a flow

The argument begins with a distinction that is easy to blur. Wealth is a stock measured at a
moment: a home net of its mortgage, a business stake, a pension account, a bond portfolio.
Income is a flow measured across a period: wages, profits, rents, and interest received over
a year. A large stock can generate a stream of income without the owner performing new work
that year. That is not an accusation. It is the accounting fact that lets ownership matter
alongside labor.

Keeping the two measures separate makes better questions possible. Is a rise in national
income reaching workers, owners, or both? Is a household gaining from a salary, an asset
price, or an inheritance? The answers lead to different policy and personal conclusions.

## The wealth-to-income ratio changes the scale

The same annual return has a different social weight when the underlying asset stock is
small than when it is several years of national income. Piketty follows the ratio of private
wealth to national income because it gives that scale a common language. If private wealth
is large relative to one year's national income, ownership claims can shape more of the
economy's future income. The ratio does not announce who owns the wealth, but it tells us why
the distribution of ownership deserves attention.

That ratio also avoids a common shortcut. A country can be richer in aggregate while access
to wealth remains narrow. Growth and broad ownership are separate questions.

## The twentieth century was not a self-correction

The book's long view resists a comforting story in which mature economies automatically
become more equal. In the wealthy countries Piketty studies, the great compression of the
mid-twentieth century followed war, depression, inflation, destruction, nationalization,
capital controls, and more progressive taxation. Those events changed both the amount of
private wealth and the rules around it. The later rise in concentration is therefore not a
simple return to a timeless equilibrium, nor proof that one variable explains every country.

The useful lesson is historical: distribution is built through institutions and events.
Policy can change the after-tax return, who has access to assets, the cost of education, the
strength of inheritance, and the share of income available to save.

## Returns become concentration through saving

The shorthand r > g can sound as if any high return immediately creates inequality. It does
not. Returns have to remain with owners, and owners have to save enough of them for the
asset base to expand. Debt, consumption, taxes, gifts, losses, and dilution can all change
the path. Yet the loop matters because a larger base can produce a larger return next year.
If the highest-wealth households also save more, or obtain better net returns, the loop has
more force at the top than elsewhere.

This is why a one-year market gain is not the claim. The concern is a compounding pattern
that can operate over decades and generations.

## Distribution depends on more than the average return

An average can hide the machinery that separates households. Portfolio scale can affect the
fees paid, the advice available, the ability to wait through a loss, and access to private
opportunities. Labor income, housing, inheritance, and tax rules also feed into who can
accumulate. Piketty's framework is most useful when it opens this system rather than when it
is treated as a single-cause verdict.

For public choices, that means asking how a rule changes each connection. A tax on an
inheritance, affordable education, tenant protections, employee ownership, transparent asset
reporting, and progressive taxes act on different parts of the map. They cannot be judged by
the slogan alone.

## The Model

The model compares two rates over a long horizon. **r** is the net return earned by capital:
profits, rents, interest, dividends, and gains after the relevant costs. **g** is the growth
of the economy, shaped by productivity and population. When r is persistently higher than g,
past wealth can grow faster than total income. That creates pressure toward concentration if
ownership, saving, and returns are already unequal. It does not predict a fixed future, and
it does not say wages, innovation, or policy are irrelevant. It names the background
arithmetic that makes those choices matter.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Read a claim as stock, flow, or rate">
When an inequality claim appears, sort its terms before judging it. Is it about wealth at a
date, income over a year, or a rate of change? Then ask which population and which assets
are included. This small discipline prevents an annual wage statistic from standing in for
ownership.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Map one compounding loop from a clear perspective">
Pick a claim or obligation you understand, such as housing equity, a retirement account, a
family business, or a student loan. State whose balance sheet you are using: a student loan
is a liability to the borrower and an asset to the lender. Then write down what produces the
return or cost, what is retained or repaid, and what rule changes the next round. The point is
not to make a prediction. It is to see where the loop can be strengthened, interrupted, or
shared.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Ask who can save">
For any proposal that asks households to invest more, add two questions: who has an initial
surplus, and who can survive a loss or a long wait? This separates advice for an individual
with spare cash from a story about the whole distribution.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Compare policies by their connection">
Choose a policy you favor or oppose, then name the link it changes: pre-tax income, asset
ownership, inheritance, net return, or saving capacity. A clear mechanism makes disagreement
more useful than a debate over whether inequality matters in the abstract.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
The formula is a pressure, not an inequality machine that runs without conditions. Piketty's
own later account distinguishes r > g from a complete explanation of wealth distribution.
Savings, unequal returns, labor income, taxation, war, asset prices, debt, and institutions
all change the result. The data also deserve humility: wealth is hard to observe, especially
at the top and across borders, and the World Inequality Database revises its series as new
sources and methods arrive. Treat the book as a durable way to frame questions about
ownership, not as permission to infer a country's future from two letters.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

Growth alone does not tell you whether prosperity is becoming broadly owned. When returns on
capital persistently exceed economic growth, and an already narrow group retains enough to
reinvest, inequality can compound. The counterweights are historical and political, which
means they can be chosen.
