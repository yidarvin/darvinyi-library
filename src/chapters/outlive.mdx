## The thesis

Attia asks medicine to care about the quality of the extra years, not merely their count. His
case is for an earlier, more personal form of prevention: identify meaningful risks before they
become a diagnosis, build a body that can keep doing ordinary work, and adjust the plan as new
information arrives. The aim is not immortality. It is to widen the portion of life in which you
remain capable, connected, and able to choose.

## Why it matters

Modern care is exceptionally good at responding to an emergency. The difficulty is that many
long-running conditions do their early work quietly. By the time a problem announces itself,
some options may be narrower and some capacity may already be gone. That makes prevention less
like a one-time checkup and more like maintaining a machine whose future failures are expensive.

## Key ideas

### Lifespan is a count; healthspan is the part you can use

Living longer and living well can overlap, but they are not the same goal. A person can add years
while losing the ability to walk far, lift a bag, think clearly, sleep well, or participate in the
people and work they care about. Healthspan gives the ambition a more demanding test: would the
extra time still feel like yours? It shifts attention from the date at the end of life to the
capacity available before it.

### The useful moment to act often arrives before the symptom

Risk is not fate, and it rarely moves in a clean line. Still, many harmful processes have a long
quiet phase before they become an acute event or a disabling diagnosis. Attia's provocation is to
take that quiet phase seriously. The point is neither to scan everything nor to panic at every
number. It is to ask which risks are credible for this person, what would change a decision, and
when it makes sense to look again.

### A few chronic fronts deserve sustained attention

The book groups much of late-life illness into cardiovascular disease, cancer, neurodegenerative
disease, and metabolic disease. These are not a complete map of human suffering, and one person
can face other urgent risks. The grouping is useful because the fronts are not isolated. Blood
pressure, movement, smoking, sleep, family history, care access, and metabolic health can matter
across more than one of them. A good plan looks for connections without pretending that one habit
solves every problem.

### Exercise is a reserve of options

Attia gives exercise unusual weight because it is both a direct practice and a way to hold onto
future choices. Endurance helps with sustained effort. Strength helps with force and everyday
loads. Stability helps make movement reliable under changing conditions. None is a magic shield,
and fitness measures in long-term studies are associations rather than a personal forecast. But a
larger reserve can make an illness, a fall, a move, or a demanding trip less likely to turn into a
loss of independence.

### Start with a future task, then train backward

Abstract goals make it hard to know what to practice. A more concrete question is, "What would I
like an ordinary day to ask of me later?" Carrying groceries, getting up from the floor, traveling
with a friend, and playing on the ground with a child all imply particular kinds of capacity.
Choose a few that matter, name the abilities behind them, practice a manageable version now, and
revisit the target as life changes. The method makes training a preparation for a life, not a
contest with a spreadsheet.

## The Model

Medicine 3.0 is Attia's name for a shift in posture. Medicine 2.0 waits for evidence of disease,
then applies a proven treatment. Medicine 3.0 tries to make earlier, individualized choices under
uncertainty: it combines family history, current measures, preferences, and changing evidence to
reduce risk before disease is obvious. The contrast is a useful prompt, not a replacement for
ordinary care. Good acute care and prevention are partners, and prevention still needs a reasoned
case for each test, treatment, and tradeoff.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Write a small future-capacity list">
Choose three activities you would like to keep doing in later decades. Make them concrete enough
to picture: carry a suitcase upstairs, take a long walk with a friend, get down to the floor and
back up. For each one, name the likely demands, such as endurance, leg strength, grip, balance, or
confidence. This is a direction for practice, not a fitness test.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Build the weekly floor before chasing the ceiling">
If activity is safe for you, begin with a weekly movement routine you can repeat, then add both
aerobic and muscle-strengthening work gradually. Public-health guidance gives adults a broad
baseline, but the useful starting dose depends on health, injury history, medication, disability,
and current activity. Ask a qualified clinician or exercise professional for a starting plan when
those factors make the choice unclear.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Prepare a prevention conversation">
Bring family history, current medications, past test results, symptoms, and the questions you
actually care about to a routine visit. Ask which evidence-based preventive services apply to you,
what benefit and harm each one carries, and what result would change the next decision. A longer
test list is not automatically a better plan.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Treat emotional health as part of the plan">
Put one durable support on the calendar: a difficult conversation, time with someone trustworthy,
therapy, a support group, or a clinical visit. The model is too narrow if it tracks blood markers
and ignores isolation, stress, addiction, grief, or depression. Seek urgent help for thoughts of
self-harm or danger to others.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
Medicine 3.0 is a framing device, not a validated protocol. Earlier testing can find actionable
risk, but it can also create false alarms, incidental findings, expense, and treatment cascades.
Preventive recommendations apply differently by age, sex, family history, symptoms, and baseline
risk, so a personalized plan needs shared decision-making with a qualified clinician. The book's
interest in longevity therapies can also outpace evidence. A 2025 review concluded that human data
do not yet establish rapamycin as a proven treatment to delay aging in healthy older adults. Do
not use this page to self-prescribe drugs, supplements, scans, fasting regimens, or extreme
training.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

Aim to arrive in later life with choices. Protect healthspan by building capacity, taking credible
risks seriously before they become urgent, and revising the plan with evidence and the clinicians
who know your situation.
