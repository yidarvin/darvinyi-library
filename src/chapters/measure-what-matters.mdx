## The thesis

OKRs give an organization a way to turn intent into a visible operating agreement. An
objective says what meaningful change the team is trying to create. Key results say what
evidence will show that the change happened, within a stated period. The strength of the
pair is not the template. It is the conversation it forces: choose a few priorities, make
success observable, let connected teams see the same commitments, and learn from the score
before the work is over.

## Why it matters

Organizations rarely lack for useful work. They struggle to decide which useful work is
important now, and to notice early when motion is not producing the intended effect. A goal
system can make those tradeoffs public. It cannot decide the strategy, but it can stop a
strategy from dissolving into private interpretations and an overfull project list.

## Focus is a choice to leave work out

An objective is not a filing cabinet for every desirable improvement. It is a bet on the
change that deserves disproportionate attention in the next cycle. That makes the list
short enough to remember and consequential enough to affect ordinary decisions. A team that
has named three true priorities can see the fourth request for what it is: a tradeoff to
make, not a free addition.

Focus is therefore less about motivational wording than subtraction. The useful question is
not whether a candidate goal sounds good. It is whether it is important enough to displace
something already on the page. If nothing would move aside, the list is not yet a set of
priorities.

## An objective points; key results supply the proof

The objective should be a compact description of the change worth pursuing, clear enough to
give a team a common destination. It is qualitative because it has to carry meaning. Key
results are different. They establish the finish line: a particular threshold, date, rate,
or condition that an informed observer can verify. They are not a list of activities the
team hopes to complete.

That distinction catches a common failure. "Run a campaign" can be work that matters, but it
does not tell anyone whether the campaign changed the problem. A result such as a measured
increase in qualified demand is harder to write because it asks for a theory of what would
count as progress. That difficulty is part of the value.

## Alignment is a map of related commitments

Publishing goals does not make every team do the same work. It gives them a way to see where
their work meets. A product team may own activation, a support team may own response quality,
and a marketing team may own the promise made before a customer arrives. Separate objectives
can still be aligned when their dependencies are explicit and their key results do not reward
one group for making another group's work worse.

The point is coordination, not a rigid cascade. Some objectives must come from leaders,
because they express a strategic decision. Others should originate near the work, where a
team sees an opportunity or risk that the top level cannot. The test is whether people can
trace a local commitment to a shared purpose and raise a conflict before it becomes a missed
deadline.

## A score is useful only when it changes the next move

Writing a goal at the start of a quarter is not the practice. The practice is a cadence of
checking the measures, naming what has changed, and deciding whether the current plan still
makes sense. A missed key result is information. It may reveal weak execution, an unreliable
measure, a mistaken assumption, or a priority that no longer deserves the same resources.

This makes tracking a management habit rather than a reporting ritual. The review should be
brief enough to repeat, specific enough to expose reality, and safe enough that a red score
produces help or a decision instead of defensive storytelling. End-of-cycle scoring then has
a purpose: it preserves the learning that should shape the next set of objectives.

## Stretch needs an honest label

Some goals are commitments: a team must meet them because another person, system, or safety
constraint depends on the result. Others are aspirational: a team is deliberately trying to
reach beyond its reliable range. Treating both as identical creates bad incentives. People
either sandbag the ambitious target, or hide trouble on the essential one.

The better move is to state the type of goal, the reason for the stretch, and the downside if
it is missed. An aspirational goal can invite a new approach. It should not quietly become a
performance trap. A committed goal can demand reliability. It should not be dressed up as a
moonshot simply to sound impressive.

## The Model

An OKR is a compact chain from a chosen change to evidence that the change occurred. First,
name the objective in language people can act on. Then select the few results that, together,
would make that claim credible. Give each result a threshold and a time window. Finally, use
regular check-ins to decide what to continue, change, or stop. The diagram is deliberately
simple because an OKR should fit into a working conversation, not become a second planning
system.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Choose one change that deserves the next cycle">
Write down every plausible priority for the next six to twelve weeks. Then force a ranking.
Keep only the two or three changes that would make the greatest difference if they moved.
For each item you leave out, write one sentence saying why it can wait. Share the list with
the people who will have to make the tradeoffs.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Draft an objective before you draft the task list">
Describe the change in one memorable sentence. Then ask three people close to the work what
they think it asks them to do. If their answers point in unrelated directions, make the
objective more concrete. Do not add measures yet. First make sure the destination is worth
sharing.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Find three pieces of proof">
For that objective, write three results that would let a skeptical colleague verify progress.
Give each a starting point, target, deadline, and owner. Cross out anything that only names
an activity, meeting, or deliverable. Keep the work plan separately, where it can change
without changing the definition of success.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Install a fifteen-minute check-in">
Once each week, look at the key results together. Record the current score, the fact behind
it, and one decision: continue, change the plan, ask for help, or stop. At the end of the
cycle, score the set and write what the measures taught you before setting the next one.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
OKRs do not create a strategy, and a precise score does not make the chosen direction wise.
Use them after a real choice about customers, capabilities, and constraints has been made.
They also should not replace a health dashboard. A key result is a measure selected for a
change; a standing metric may still be needed to catch damage the goal does not see. Pair
quantity with quality when speed, revenue, or volume could create harm elsewhere. Finally,
do not use an aspirational score as a crude individual compensation formula. If missing a
stretch goal carries a direct personal penalty, the system teaches caution and metric gaming
instead of honest ambition.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

An OKR is not a grand statement with numbers attached. It is a short, public agreement about
which change matters now, what evidence will count, and when the team will look at reality
together.

Good Strategy Bad Strategy clarifies the strategic diagnosis that should come before an OKR,
Drive examines the human conditions behind commitment, and Essentialism offers the discipline
of choosing what does not belong on the list.
