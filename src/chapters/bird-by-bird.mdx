## The thesis

Anne Lamott's answer to the terror of a blank page is to reduce the unit of work. A book is
too large to write in one sitting, and a perfect sentence is too high a bar for a first
attempt. Choose one scene, memory, image, or question. Put down an imperfect version while
the inner judge is temporarily off duty. Then return as an editor. The work becomes possible
because discovery and judgment no longer have to happen in the same moment.

## Why it matters

Writing can look mysterious when seen only in finished form. The public sees the clean
argument, the vivid scene, or the published book. It does not see the hesitant pages that
made those things available. Lamott makes that hidden middle ordinary. Her method gives a
writer a way to proceed without pretending that uncertainty, envy, fear, or a weak first
attempt will disappear first.

## Make the assignment small enough to enter

Overwhelm often arrives through scale. "Write the novel" asks for a finished world before a
writer has selected a door into it. A smaller prompt creates a boundary: describe the room
after the argument, follow one person through one errand, recall the sound that starts a
memory. The small piece is not a consolation prize. It is a real unit of composition, one
that can reveal the larger work's voice, stakes, and next question.

## Let the first pass be private and unruly

The book's famous permission for bad first drafts is not a claim that careless writing is the
goal. It separates two useful jobs. Early pages are a place to find what you think, remember,
and care about, including the false starts and excess that later work will remove. A polished
page asks for selection, proportion, and a reader's attention. Asking for all of that before
there is material on the page hands the editor an empty desk to criticize.

## Details make experience believable

General labels report an opinion. Particular sensory evidence lets a reader arrive at one.
Rather than announcing that a kitchen was sad, a draft can notice the cold coffee ring, the
calendar left on the wall, or the person avoiding the chair by the window. The point is not to
pile up decoration. It is to select details that carry feeling, setting, and implication at
once, then trust the reader to take part in the meaning.

## Start with someone who wants something

Plot is easier to force when it begins as a list of things that should happen. Lamott instead
puts attention on a person with a desire, a limitation, and a situation that presses back.
Choices then create consequences, which change the next choice. This gives a scene an engine.
The writer still has to arrange and revise, but character stops being furniture moved through
a prewritten sequence and becomes the source of the sequence's pressure.

## Revision needs distance and another pair of eyes

A writer is too close to a new draft to know what it communicates. Time creates enough
distance to hear its repetitions and missing steps. A trusted reader adds a second kind of
evidence: where attention brightened, where meaning was lost, and what the page seems to be
about. Feedback is not a command to obey. It is information to compare with the work's own
intention. The writer remains responsible for the next decision.

## The Model

"Shitty first drafts" is deliberately blunt permission to begin without performing mastery.
It is a model of sequence, not an excuse to stop at the rough version. First, get a private
draft onto the page. Next, find the material with energy and remove what merely filled space.
Then revise for the person who will read it. A new draft can restart the loop, but each pass
has one job and therefore a workable standard.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Choose a scene, not a project">
Write the name of a project that feels too large to begin. Under it, name one piece that fits
in twenty minutes: a remembered room, a difficult phone call, a list of objects from a trip,
or the moment before a decision. Set a timer and write only that piece.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Give the first pass a protected rule">
For one session, keep moving forward. Do not delete whole paragraphs, search for a better
word, or reread from the beginning. Mark a problem with brackets if you must, then continue.
When the timer ends, close the document before deciding what the draft means.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Trade one abstraction for evidence">
Find one sentence that names a feeling, such as "the meeting was tense." Replace or follow it
with one visible action, sound, object, or bodily response. Keep only the detail that changes
how the reader understands the moment.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Ask for one kind of feedback">
After a day away from a short draft, give it to one trusted reader with a narrow question:
where did your attention sharpen, and where did you get lost? Listen without defending the
page. Write down the answers, then decide privately which revision serves the piece.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
The permission to draft badly is permission for a stage, not a defense of publishing work that
has not been revised. The book's generous, personal voice can also make a solitary writing
life look more available than it is. Regular practice is valuable, but a schedule must make
room for paid work, disability, caregiving, health, and unequal access to quiet, time, and
feedback. Small assignments reduce the intimidation of a project. They do not remove those
conditions or replace the craft of sustained revision.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

When the work feels impossible, lower the size of the next assignment and lower the standard
of the first pass. Make honest material first. Shape it after there is something real to see.
