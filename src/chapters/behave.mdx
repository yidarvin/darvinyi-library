## The thesis

Robert Sapolsky asks a disarming question: what happened before a person did that? His answer
is never a single thing. An action is made in the present by a nervous system shaped by recent
sleep, stress, hormones, learning, childhood, social rules, and a much longer biological past.
Explanation does not make behavior harmless or unavoidable. It makes a shallow story about
character less tempting, and a better response more possible.

## Why it matters

We are quick to explain a stranger's worst moment as a durable trait and our own as a bad day.
That shortcut feels efficient because the act is visible and its causes are not. *Behave* asks
for a wider frame. It gives neither a biological excuse nor a lecture about personal virtue. It
asks what conditions made this response likely, which conditions could change, and where a
person or institution can act before the next moment arrives.

## Key ideas

### The act is the tip, not the whole explanation

An insult, a generous choice, a violent outburst, or a pause before speaking is the visible end
of a long chain. The seconds before matter: attention, threat detection, memory, and inhibition
are active. So do less immediate conditions, such as fatigue, a stressful week, learned habits,
and a setting that rewards one response over another. Going backward does not discover a single
root cause. It reveals a stack of influences that jointly made one action easier than its rivals.
The practical question shifts from "What kind of person is this?" to "What was this system set
up to do?"

### A brain is a negotiation, not a tiny commander

It is tempting to speak as if one rational self sits at the controls while unruly emotions press
buttons around it. The brain does not work that way. Perception, memory, valuation, bodily
signals, and control systems continually influence one another. A frightened person may notice
threat sooner. A hungry person may value immediate relief more strongly. A practiced musician
can execute a skilled movement without narrating each step. The point is not that deliberation
is powerless. It is that deliberation is one participant in a changing network, and it works
best when the surrounding conditions give it room.

### Hormones change the question; they do not supply the answer

Hormones are often turned into cartoon causes: testosterone makes aggression, oxytocin makes
kindness, cortisol makes panic. Sapolsky's better lesson is conditional. A hormone can alter
what feels salient, how strongly a challenge registers, or which social goal is pursued. Its
effect depends on the person, the situation, other biological signals, and what the environment
makes possible. Biology is real without being a script. Treating it as a script hides the very
context that tells us what a biological change will mean.

### Yesterday is still in the room

The causal history of an action does not begin in infancy. A short night of sleep, a threat a
few minutes ago, a week of repeated strain, or a recent experience of being supported can alter
the next decision. Stress can narrow attention and make a familiar response easier to repeat,
though people vary and no one response is guaranteed. This is useful because some near causes
are reachable. A pause, food, sleep, a less provocative meeting, or time to recover may not
solve a deep problem, but they can change the conditions under which a choice is made.

### The boundary of "us" can move

Humans readily form groups, but the membership of a group is neither fixed nor purely
biological. A shared task can make strangers feel like a team. A threat can make a social line
feel harder. A larger identity can move the boundary outward, from a neighborhood or nation to
a profession, a species, or a common future. This flexibility matters because loyalty is not the
opposite of cruelty. People can be deeply generous within a circle while treating outsiders as
less worthy. The work is to notice which circle is organizing the moment and whether it has been
drawn too tightly.

## The Model

Sapolsky's model is a set of nested timescales, read from the center outward. The action happens
now, but its immediate neural conditions are already shaped by the body's current state, recent
experience, development, culture, and evolutionary history. These are not five competing
explanations from which to pick a winner. They are levels of the same explanation. Start nearest
to the act when you need to intervene, then keep moving outward until the explanation includes
the conditions that made the near cause likely.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Describe the sequence before judging it">
After a difficult interaction, write four plain facts: what happened just before, what changed in
your body, what story or memory arrived, and what you did next. Keep adjectives about character
out of the first pass. You are making a sequence that can be examined, not a case for acquittal.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Find one near condition you can change">
Choose a recurring behavior and look for one condition within reach: an overloaded calendar, a
conversation held too late, a notification that keeps provoking you, or a missing meal or break.
Change it for a week. This does not claim the condition is the whole cause. It tests whether a
small change in the setup makes a different response easier.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Widen the circle before a group decision">
Before a team, family, or online argument, name who counts as "us" in the discussion and who
will bear the cost of the decision. Add one absent perspective to the room, even if only by
asking what it would need. A wider frame does not settle every conflict, but it interrupts the
ease of treating outsiders as scenery.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Match responsibility to the level of cause">
When a problem repeats, separate what one person can practice from what a group or institution
must change. A person may need sleep, treatment, skill, or a pause. A workplace may need safer
hours, clearer rules, or fewer humiliating incentives. Responsibility becomes more useful when
it includes the conditions that distribute risk and power.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
Explaining behavior is not excusing it, and biological influence is not biological destiny.
Evidence at every level is probabilistic, often drawn from narrow settings, and can be revised.
For example, a <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35201818/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2022 randomized study of testosterone</a> found competitive choices depended on both cortisol and status cues, not testosterone alone. A <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22101494/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">controlled acute-stress study</a> found impaired flexible goal switching in one sample, not a universal diagnosis of how stressed people act. Use the framework to resist lazy explanations and to locate changeable conditions. Do not use it to predict an individual, erase accountability, or turn a research result into a label.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

No behavior comes from nowhere. Look for the immediate conditions first, then widen the frame
until the body, history, social setting, and long inheritance are all visible. That fuller story
is where better prevention and fairer responsibility begin.
