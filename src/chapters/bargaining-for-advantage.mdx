## The thesis

Good bargaining is less about becoming a harder person than about becoming a better prepared
one. Shell treats a negotiation as a problem with several sources of strength: clear goals,
reliable standards, knowledge of what matters to both sides, a workable relationship, and an
alternative that makes walking away possible. Knowing your natural style helps you use those
sources without performing a personality that collapses under pressure.

## Why it matters

People often enter a negotiation with only a preferred result in mind. That leaves them
vulnerable to a confident opening number, an invented deadline, or their own discomfort with
conflict. Preparation changes the experience. Instead of improvising a response to every
move, you can tell what the other side is asking for, what you need to learn, which terms are
actually flexible, and when no deal is the better decision.

## Start with the negotiator you actually are

Some people seek agreement quickly. Others enjoy a contest, avoid tension until too late, or
need more time to think before speaking. None of these tendencies is a permanent verdict on
how well someone can negotiate. They become useful or costly depending on the situation. A
cooperative style can build trust but may need a firmer limit. A competitive style can defend
an important interest but may miss a trade that would enlarge the deal. The practical task is
to notice your default under stress, then prepare a counterweight before the conversation
starts.

## Turn a wish into a decision rule

“I want a good deal” gives you nothing to steer by when the other side presents a concrete
offer. A useful goal has a specific target, a reason it is defensible, and a point below which
the deal stops making sense. It also has an alternative: what you will do if an agreement does
not arrive. Those four elements keep aspiration from turning into fantasy and caution from
turning into a reflexive concession. They let you evaluate the whole package, rather than
accepting one attractive term while a damaging condition slips past.

## Look below the demand for the interests doing the work

An announced price, date, or condition is an answer. It does not tell you what problem that
answer is meant to solve. A buyer pressing on price may be protecting a budget, a reputation
for fairness, or the ability to obtain internal approval. A seller holding a date may be
managing capacity or risk. Ask questions that make those pressures discussable, and explain
your own constraints with comparable clarity. The aim is not to guess motives correctly on
the first try. It is to replace a narrow fight over one answer with a fuller map of the
interests that an agreement would have to satisfy.

## Let standards carry part of the argument

Pressure can win a concession, but it rarely explains why that concession is fair or durable.
When the parties disagree about a number, a deadline, or a division of responsibility, look
for a reference they can both inspect: a market range, a published policy, an independent
assessment, a comparable deal, or a rule that fits the case. A standard does not eliminate
judgment. It gives judgment something outside either person's preference to work against.
Use it openly enough that the other side can question it, and be willing to revise it if it
does not actually match the situation.

## Treat the relationship as working infrastructure

A relationship is not a reason to accept a bad deal. It is the condition that makes candid
exchange, reliable follow-through, and repair after a disagreement more possible. In a
one-time transaction, that may mean setting a clear, respectful way to share information and
make commitments. In an ongoing relationship, it also means noticing the history each side
brings into the room. Before negotiating, consider what has built confidence, what has damaged
it, who needs to be heard, and how an agreement will be carried out after the meeting. That
preparation protects both the substance of the deal and the ability to work together afterward.

## Build leverage before you need to show it

Leverage is not just a loud threat or a larger title. It grows when you have choices, useful
information, enough time to decide, and a credible ability to carry out the path you name.
That is why preparation matters before the meeting. Find other suppliers, buyers, jobs,
funding sources, or ways to postpone the decision. Learn who approves the deal and what
commitments constrain them. Do not confuse leverage with bluffing. A claim that cannot be
executed invites a test, while a real alternative lets you state your boundary calmly and
keep negotiating without desperation.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Make a one-page negotiation brief">
For an upcoming conversation, write the target you want, the minimum you can responsibly
accept, and the alternative you will use if there is no agreement. Add the assumptions behind
each line. If you cannot explain why the target is reasonable or how the alternative would
work, the brief has found work to do before the meeting.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Prepare questions for the hidden problem">
Write the demand you expect to hear, then list three possible interests it could protect.
For each one, draft a neutral question that tests it: “What would that date need to make
possible?” or “What risk would this term remove for you?” Treat the list as a set of
hypotheses, not a private diagnosis of the other person.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Choose a standard before the dispute">
Find one external reference for each important term, such as a published salary range,
comparable contract, policy, cost estimate, or independent opinion. Bring the source, not
just the conclusion. Then ask yourself what evidence would make you change your view. A
standard is useful only if it constrains both sides.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Prepare the working relationship">
Before the conversation, write three notes: the history that matters, the communication
condition that will help each side speak plainly, and the first repair step if the discussion
turns tense. For example, agree who needs to be consulted, how tentative terms will be
recorded, and when either person can pause to check an assumption. Do not perform warmth. Make
it easier for both sides to exchange information and keep commitments.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="05" title="Strengthen one source of leverage">
Pick the weak link in your preparation: too few options, too little information, too much
time pressure, or no authority to act. Take one concrete step before the conversation, such
as obtaining another quote, learning the approval path, moving a deadline, or arranging a
backup plan. State no threat that you are not prepared to carry out.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
Knowing your style should not become an excuse to stay predictable, and preparation should
not become a script used to corner someone. Culture, hierarchy, gender, safety, and legal
obligations shape what can be asked for and how freely a person can say no. Better questions
and a stronger alternative cannot repair coercion or make an exploitative deal fair. When
power is badly unequal or conduct is unsafe, the right next step may be outside support, a
formal process, or leaving the negotiation.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

Enter a negotiation knowing more than what you hope to get. Know what matters to both sides,
which standard could justify a deal, and what you will do if no deal is good enough. That
preparation lets you negotiate as yourself, with less need to bluff, rush, or surrender.
