## The thesis

Harari treats the decline of some old mass threats as a change in the human agenda. A
species that once concentrated on surviving hunger, infection, and violence may turn its
resources toward longer lives, engineered bodies, greater pleasure, and more capable
machines. The book's unsettling claim is that these projects can also displace the human
being as the final judge. If algorithms become better at prediction and allocation, a
culture organized around human feelings and choices may begin to hand authority to systems
that process more data than any person can hold.

## Why it matters

This is not a manual for predicting a date when machines take over. It is a way to notice
where decisions are already being delegated: a recommendation decides what appears next, a
score opens or closes an opportunity, and a medical model changes which question a clinician
asks. The book makes power visible at the point where convenience becomes authority.

## Success changes the list of problems

A society's ambitions are shaped by what it has learned to manage. Harari begins from a
historical shift: famine, epidemics, and war remain grave realities, but many states now
treat them as risks to contain rather than fixed laws of life. That success can redirect
attention. The new projects are enhancement projects, such as extending healthy life,
raising cognitive capacity, and designing organisms rather than merely adapting to them.
They are not automatically humane because they sound medical or technical. The same
capacity can repair a body, sort bodies into classes, or make a costly preference feel
inevitable.

## Humanism puts experience in the judge's chair

The book calls the modern authority of inner experience humanism. In this picture, a good
life is not found by obedience to a cosmic order. It is assessed through felt experience,
individual choice, and human rights. Harari's point is that this arrangement made sense
when people were the most capable interpreters of their own preferences. A system that can
infer a person's sleep, health, taste, attention, and habits may claim it can decide better.
The shift is dangerous precisely because it can arrive as help. Advice becomes default;
the default becomes a decision made elsewhere.

## The self can be measured without being settled

Harari presses a reductionist metaphor: organisms can be studied as information-processing
systems. Biology does use signals, feedback, and chemical mechanisms, and measurement can
reveal patterns a person misses. But a useful description is not a complete moral account.
A biometric trace may register stress without explaining its cause. A preference model can
predict a click without deciding whether the click was wise, free, or worth pursuing. The
book is strongest here as a prompt to separate what a system can measure from what it is
entitled to decide.

## Enhancement can turn equality into a distribution problem

New capabilities do not spread by magic. They depend on capital, institutions, expertise,
and political choices. Harari worries that a technology that improves bodies or minds could
create a sharper divide than earlier economic gaps, especially if a small group controls
the tools and their benefits accumulate. The relevant question is not whether an advance is
good in the abstract. Ask who can access it, who is exposed to its mistakes, who owns the
resulting data, and what recourse exists when a classification harms someone.

## Data gains force when it joins a feedback loop

Data is not power because a spreadsheet exists. It becomes power when collection feeds a
model, the model guides a decision, and that decision changes the behavior that produces the
next round of data. A map can route a driver, a feed can shape attention, and a risk score
can change who receives a loan or a visit from an inspector. Each loop can be useful. It
also concentrates influence in the choice of target, the data allowed in, and the mechanism
for correction. The person affected by a loop needs more than a result. They need a way to
see it, contest it, and change it.

## The Model

Dataism is Harari's name for a possible reversal of authority. Instead of treating human
experience as the source of meaning and systems as tools, it treats the efficient movement
and analysis of information as the central good. That is a diagnosis of a cultural tendency,
not an instruction to obey data. The model helps locate the point of responsibility: people
choose the sensors, the categories, the objective, the action, and the appeals process.
Leaving any of those choices unnamed makes an institution look more neutral than it is.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Name the decision behind the recommendation">
The next time an app recommends, ranks, routes, or filters something for you, write down
the actual decision it is making. Then ask which outcome it appears to optimize: speed,
revenue, engagement, safety, or something else. A recommendation is easier to evaluate when
its target is stated plainly.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Trace one feedback loop">
Pick a system at work, school, or home that collects information. Identify what it records,
what action follows, and how that action changes future records. Look for the missing voice:
the person affected who cannot see, correct, or appeal the process.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Keep a human checkpoint">
For a consequential choice, decide in advance what should never be delegated without review.
It might be a hiring rejection, a medical recommendation, a credit decision, or a classroom
placement. Define the evidence a person should inspect and the reason they must be able to
give.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Ask an access question before an enhancement question">
When you hear about a new health, education, or AI capability, start with distribution. Who
can use it, who pays for it, who bears errors, and who can opt out? This moves the discussion
from novelty to the conditions of a fair public choice.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
Read <em>Homo Deus</em> as a deliberately sharp future scenario, not as a timetable. Its
opening contrast between old threats and new ambitions can make famine and infectious disease
sound finished when their burdens remain grave and uneven. The 2025 <a href="https://www.fao.org/agrifood-economics/publications/detail/en/c/1740904/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">UN food-security report</a> says the world is not on track to end hunger by 2030, and the <a href="https://apps.who.int/gb/ebwha/pdf_files/WHA77/A77_16-en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">World Health Organization</a> reports that communicable diseases still kill millions each year. Nor is Dataism a scientific law or a settled description of mind. It is Harari's name for a tendency worth resisting or redirecting. His own <a href="https://www.ynharari.com/book/homo-deus/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">book page</a> carries an errata notice. The lasting question is narrower and more useful: when a system knows a great deal, what authority should it have over a person?
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

Power does not move to data by itself. It moves when people treat a measurement or prediction
as a reason to decide without naming the values, limits, and accountability that should still
belong to people.
