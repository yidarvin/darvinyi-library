## The thesis

Messages disappear less often because they are false or badly delivered than because they
ask the audience to retain an expert's whole mental model. Chip and Dan Heath argue that an
idea becomes easier to remember and repeat when its structure respects how attention and
memory work. Give people a compact center, interrupt their expectations, make the claim
touchable and testable, connect it to a human concern, and let a story show the next move.
The framework is a craft tool: it helps a useful idea survive the trip from the person who
knows it to the person who must understand it.

## Why it matters

Most important work begins with an information imbalance. The person explaining a policy,
proposal, diagnosis, or design has spent hours inside its details. The listener has not. A
deck can be accurate, a meeting can be polished, and the core idea can still leave the room
without changing a later choice. The problem is not a shortage of information. It is the gap
between knowing a subject deeply and giving another person a usable way into it.

## Find the point that can travel without you

Simplicity is not the same as stripping away every qualification. It means deciding what the
audience should retain if all the supporting material falls away. A useful message has a
center of gravity: one priority, one contrast, or one decision rule that can guide judgment
when the expert is absent. The details still matter, but they should serve that center rather
than compete with it. If a sentence cannot survive a hurried retelling, it may be background
masquerading as the point.

## Use surprise to open an honest question

Attention wakes up when a pattern breaks. The useful move is not a stunt or a random twist.
It is a detail that exposes a gap between what someone assumes and what they need to know.
That gap creates a question, and a good explanation earns the attention it borrowed by
answering it. In practice, lead with a counterintuitive result, a revealing comparison, or a
concrete consequence. Then resolve the tension with the idea that changes how the audience
sees the problem.

## Replace category words with things people can picture

Abstract language travels badly because different people supply different meanings. Terms
such as quality, alignment, safety, or innovation can sound agreeable while leaving no shared
image of what to do. Concrete language gives the claim handles. Name the customer who waits,
the screen where the choice happens, the count that changes, or the behavior a teammate can
observe. Concrete does not mean simplistic. It gives a complicated idea a stable reference
point so people can discuss and apply it without guessing at the vocabulary.

## Build belief from evidence the audience can inspect

A claim becomes more credible when the listener can see why it should be trusted. Sometimes
that means an independent source or a recognized expert. Often it means a demonstration,
plain numbers, a before-and-after comparison, or a test that someone can run for themselves.
The strongest choice depends on the decision at hand. The point is to move beyond asking for
faith. Give people a way to check the claim against something outside the speaker's confidence.

## Make the stakes human and specific

People rarely care about a category in the abstract for very long. They care when they can
locate a consequence in a person, a relationship, a responsibility, or a value they already
hold. This is not permission to manipulate sentiment. It is a demand for precision. Instead
of saying a delay affects service quality, show what a delay costs the person waiting. Instead
of saying a policy supports fairness, identify the choice that becomes fairer and for whom.
Feeling is most useful when it clarifies what is at stake and points toward a responsible act.

## Let a story rehearse the next choice

Stories do more than decorate a message. A well-chosen scene lets people mentally try a
response before the situation arrives. It has a person facing a recognizable obstacle, a
choice under pressure, and a result that makes the consequence visible. That structure
teaches without turning the lesson into a slogan. When listeners later meet a similar moment,
the story can supply both a memory cue and a possible course of action.

## The Model

SUCCESs is a checklist for shaping a message before it has to compete for attention. The six
qualities are simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, and story-based. They are
not six steps to apply in a rigid order, nor must every message maximize every quality. Read
them as connected questions. What is the point? What will make someone pause? What can they
picture, trust, care about, and retell? A stronger answer on one spoke can support the others.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Write the one-line center">
Choose a message you need another person to remember. Write the decision, contrast, or rule
in one sentence of ordinary language. Put the supporting details on a separate page. If the
sentence tries to cover every objection, shorten it until someone could repeat it after one
hearing.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Trade one abstraction for an observation">
Circle the broadest word in your draft: perhaps trust, efficiency, quality, or growth. Replace
it with one observable event, person, number, or choice. Read the new sentence to someone
outside the project and ask what they picture. Revise until the answer is close to the scene
you intended.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Create a question worth answering">
List the assumption your audience is likely to bring. Find one truthful fact that complicates
it. Use that fact near the opening, then make sure the next paragraph answers the question it
creates. Surprise that never resolves into understanding is only noise.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Add one check and one scene">
For the central claim, name the evidence a skeptical listener could inspect. Then write a
three-sentence scene in which a real person faces the relevant choice. The first builds trust;
the second gives the message a situation in which to live.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
Stickiness is a communication property, not a moral one. Rumors and sensational claims can be
simple, vivid, emotional, and easy to repeat while still being false or harmful. The framework
therefore needs an ethical gate before a craft pass: check the claim, show uncertainty where
it belongs, and do not use a person's fear or grief as a device. It is also a diagnostic, not
a formula. A high-stakes legal, medical, or technical message may need more qualification than
a slogan can carry. The aim is a clear entry point, not the disappearance of nuance.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

Do not begin by asking how to say everything you know. Find the one idea another person needs
to carry, then give it a concrete, credible, human shape that can guide a later choice.

People focuses on the respect that helps it be heard, and Crucial Conversations applies that
discipline when the stakes make attention fragile.
