## The thesis

Kuhn's history of science rejects the picture of knowledge as a single, smooth pile of
facts. A scientific community usually works inside a shared framework that tells it what
counts as a good question, a reliable instrument, and a satisfying answer. That arrangement
makes deep, cumulative work possible. But when its failures become too consequential to
treat as local puzzles, a competing framework can reorganize the field. Progress then comes
in both modes: patient refinement and occasional reconstruction.

## Why it matters

The phrase "paradigm shift" is now used for any new app or management slogan. Kuhn meant
something more demanding. A real shift changes the standards, categories, and exemplary
problems that let a field do its work. The distinction helps separate a useful improvement
from a change in the map itself. It also explains why good evidence can take time to alter a
settled practice: evidence is interpreted inside a working system before it is allowed to
call that system into question.

## A paradigm is a working world, not a single theory

A paradigm is larger than a headline explanation. It bundles methods, instruments,
successful examples, habitual questions, standards of proof, and an educated sense of what a
solution should look like. A physicist trained with a familiar set of problems does not face
each measurement as a blank slate. The training supplies a field of possible moves.

That shared background is productive. It lets researchers divide labor, improve techniques,
and disagree about a result without first renegotiating the meaning of every term. The cost
is that the framework also directs attention. It makes some features of the world easier to
notice and others easier to treat as noise, error, or somebody else's problem.

## Normal science earns its name through concentration

Most scientific work, in Kuhn's account, is not a hunt for a new worldview. It is the hard
work of extending one that has already proved useful. Researchers make a prediction more
precise, refine an instrument, solve a difficult case, or bring a stubborn observation into
line with a known model. These are not trivial chores. A field becomes capable through this
kind of focused puzzle solving.

The framework narrows the game, and that narrowness is a feature. A problem has enough shape
to reward skill when the community agrees on the tools and on what counts as a solution. The
same discipline that produces reliable detail can, however, postpone a more radical question
about whether the rules of the game still fit the phenomenon.

## An anomaly is a mismatch, not a revolution on arrival

Every capable framework encounters awkward observations. An instrument may be misread, a
calculation may be incomplete, or a surprising case may yield once the method improves. The
first responsible response is usually to investigate the local mismatch, not to announce the
collapse of all prior knowledge. A mature field has earned that patience.

An anomaly becomes important when careful work keeps returning to the same trouble. It is no
longer a puzzle with a difficult answer, but evidence that the available rules cannot tell
researchers what answer would settle it. Its force comes from persistence, relevance, and
its relation to other problems, not from its mere novelty.

## Crisis changes the question before it changes the answer

When anomalies accumulate, the field can enter a crisis. Confidence in its usual procedures
weakens, old fixes look less convincing, and researchers become willing to try approaches
that would previously have seemed irrelevant or badly formed. This is a period of expanded
possibility, not a moment when everyone suddenly agrees on a replacement.

The visible dispute may be about a troublesome result. Beneath it lies a deeper conflict over
which concepts, measurements, and questions deserve authority. That is why a crisis can feel
confusing from inside. The group is no longer merely deciding how to solve a puzzle. It is
deciding what sort of puzzle it has.

## A new framework has to organize more than one surprise

A challenger wins attention by opening a more fruitful line of work, not by being different
for its own sake. It must handle important successes of the older framework while making
sense of problems that framework left unsettled. Adoption is social as well as evidential:
scientists compare results, learn new techniques, teach the approach, and discover whether it
generates problems worth solving.

Kuhn's point is not that evidence disappears during this process. It is that evidence does
not arrive with an instruction sheet that ranks every framework automatically. Competing
approaches may group phenomena differently and set different expectations. Shared values such
as accuracy, reach, coherence, simplicity, and future promise matter, but reasonable people
can weigh them differently while the field is still changing.

## The Model

A paradigm shift is a change in the field's operating frame. Normal science gives a community
the means to deepen a successful framework. Persistent anomalies can turn that confidence
into a crisis. A competing framework then has to earn a following by producing a more
promising program of research. Once it becomes the shared basis for new puzzles, the cycle of
normal science begins again. The sequence is a conceptual map, not a timetable or a law that
every discipline must obey.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Name the frame before debating the result">
For a recurring disagreement in your work, write down the assumption both sides are using:
what counts as success, what evidence is trusted, and what problem is being optimized. If
the answers differ, arguing over the latest data point may be premature. Start by making the
frame visible.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Keep an anomaly log">
When a process repeatedly produces an awkward exception, record it instead of explaining it
away on the spot. Include the expected result, the actual result, the condition, and the
repair attempted. Review the list after a few cycles. One exception may be noise. A pattern
of exceptions may tell you which assumption deserves a closer look.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Test a rival model as a working tool">
Choose one problem that your usual method handles poorly. Describe how a different model
would define the problem, what it would measure, and what result it would predict. Run a
small, reversible test. The aim is not to declare a revolution. It is to give an alternative
a fair chance to produce useful work.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Protect both modes of progress">
Set aside one part of a project for refinement and another for challenge. In the first,
improve the current system with clear standards. In the second, ask what the system excludes,
which recurring failure has become normal, and what a different frame would make possible.
Do not let novelty disrupt routine work, and do not let routine work make novelty invisible.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
Kuhn did not give a license to call every disagreement a paradigm shift, nor did he say that
evidence is merely a matter of taste. His historical model was shaped chiefly by physical
science and describes an influential pattern, not a universal schedule for all inquiry.
"Incommensurable" does not mean rivals cannot be compared or discussed. It marks the fact
that comparison can be difficult when terms, methods, and standards are moving together.
Later Kuhn also emphasized values used in theory choice, including accuracy, consistency,
scope, simplicity, and future fruitfulness. Those values guide judgment without supplying an
automatic decision procedure.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

Science needs settled frameworks because they make exact, shared work possible. It also needs
the discipline to notice when a framework's unresolved failures have become a reason to test
a different way of seeing.
