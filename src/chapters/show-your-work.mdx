## The thesis

Share selected, useful traces of a practice so people can find the work, learn from it, and
return to it. Over time, those traces become an archive of thinking and a path to the people
who care about the same problems.

## Why it matters

Finished work often arrives without its story. A reader sees a book, a client sees a product,
or a listener hears a song, but none of them can tell what questions, revisions, references,
or failed attempts made it possible. That gap favors people who already have attention. A
public working record gives a newer maker another route: be specific enough that someone who
cares about the same problem can recognize a fellow traveler. The aim is connection around
the work, not a performance of constant availability.

## Let the process carry some of the weight

A finished piece is a thin surface over a much larger body of activity. Notes, rejected
versions, source material, small experiments, and recurring questions all make the final
choice more intelligible. Sharing selected parts of that field can be more useful than simply
announcing that something exists. A sketch can explain a design decision. A short before-and-
after can reveal a revision. A list of sources can help another person continue the inquiry.
The public fragment does not need to expose every private draft. It needs to show a real piece
of thinking that stands on its own.

## Build a cadence small enough to keep

The useful unit of sharing is usually smaller than a launch. A photo of a prototype, a
paragraph about a problem solved, a reading note, a lesson from an error, or a link with a
sentence of context can all carry the practice forward. The point of a steady rhythm is not
to feed a platform. It is to create enough repeated signals for people to understand what you
care about and for you to notice what the work keeps teaching. Choose an interval that does
not damage the making. A modest weekly note that survives for a year is more valuable than a
daily performance that burns out in a month.

## Make your interests legible, not generic

People rarely connect to a vague claim that someone is creative, curious, or available for
work. They connect to a particular cluster of interests and a recognizable angle on it. Keep
a cabinet of material that genuinely feeds the work, then name the links you notice between
its parts. A designer might connect transit maps, accessibility, and neighborhood history. A
programmer might connect classroom tools, typography, and error messages. Those connections
are not branding decoration. They are evidence of attention, and they give others a more
precise reason to respond, recommend, or collaborate.

## Teach from the learning edge

Teaching does not require presenting yourself as the final authority. It can mean preserving
a step that was hard to find, explaining a decision you have just learned to make, or pointing
to the source that clarified a problem. This kind of teaching is generous because it reduces
someone else's search time. It is also corrective: trying to explain a method exposes what you
do not yet understand. Stay honest about the distance traveled. Report the experiment, the
constraint, and the uncertainty rather than turning a fresh lesson into a universal law.

## Offer useful signals, not demands for attention

There is a difference between making work findable and treating every interaction as a lead.
The first offers something concrete: a source, a method, a question, a finished object, or a
careful response. The second keeps asking strangers to validate, amplify, or buy before it has
given them a reason. Generosity is a practical standard here. Credit the people whose work you
share. Reply when there is a real reply to make. Let a post stand without demanding a return.
An audience is made of people with limited attention, not a resource to extract.

## Stay long enough for the conversation to deepen

Early response is noisy. A useful post may reach almost no one, while a casual fragment may
travel farther than the work you value most. Neither result is a reliable verdict on the
practice. The longer horizon is better: make, document, share, listen, and let the next round
of work be a little better informed. That loop makes an audience less like a scoreboard and
more like a community of attention. It also protects the work from the false urgency of a
single launch. Trust is built by showing up with substance again after the first burst has
passed.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Choose one visible trace">
Pick a current project and identify one artifact that would help a peer understand it: a
decision log, a reference list, a small prototype, a failed version with a lesson, or a
before-and-after. Write two sentences of context, remove anything private or misleading, and
share it in one place where interested people can find it.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Set a humane publishing rhythm">
For the next four weeks, reserve twenty minutes after one work session to save a trace of what
happened. At the end of each week, choose one trace to turn into a brief post, note, or email.
The rule is to document after making, not to interrupt the work every hour looking for content.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Teach one narrow lesson">
Write a short explanation of a problem you solved recently. Include the situation, the step
that helped, the source that informed it, and one condition under which the advice may fail.
Give the explanation to one person who is a few steps behind you, or publish it where such a
person would look.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Create a boundary before you share">
Make two lists for a current project: material that is safe to show now, and material that is
private, unfinished in a harmful way, or belongs to someone else. Keep the second list off the
publishing calendar. A clear boundary makes it easier to be open about the first list without
confusing openness with exposure.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
Showing work does not mean streaming every thought, accepting every platform's terms, or
turning a private practice into a public audition. Kleon himself has described keeping his
notebooks private when they are the messy place where he thinks. Privacy, safety, agreements
with collaborators, copyright, employment obligations, and the pressure that public feedback
can place on early experiments all set real limits. Visibility also does not distribute itself
fairly. Algorithms, existing networks, time, money, disability, language, and access shape who
gets seen. Share what you can sustain and what you are allowed to share, then judge the habit
by the relationships and learning it creates, not by a promise of reach.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

Do not wait for a finished identity or a perfect launch. Keep making, share selected evidence
of the work with generosity and clear limits, and give the right people repeated chances to
recognize what you care about.
