## The thesis

Indistractable argues that distraction is not a property of a phone, an inbox, or any
one tempting thing. It is the gap between an intention and the action that replaces it.
The useful response starts before the alert: notice the discomfort or cue that creates
the urge, decide what deserves time in advance, remove needless invitations to detour,
and set commitments that can hold when motivation drops.

## Why it matters

Most attention advice starts with the visible culprit. Close the tab, silence the
notification, buy a more restrictive tool. Those moves can help, but they leave a
question unanswered: what made the interruption appealing at that particular moment?
When an uncomfortable task, a moment of uncertainty, or an unplanned hour is doing the
pulling, the same pattern can simply find a different outlet. This book treats attention
as a design problem with an emotional starting point.

## Name the detour by the plan it displaced

A message from a friend can be connection, a game can be rest, and a scroll through a
feed can be harmless entertainment. The activity alone does not decide its meaning. The
question is whether it was the action you meant to take in that slice of time. A planned
break is part of the day. An automatic break that replaces the work, conversation, or
rest you chose is a detour.

That distinction removes a lot of empty moralizing. The goal is not permanent
seriousness, nor a ban on technology. It is the ability to spend time deliberately,
including on pleasure. A calendar creates the reference point that a bare to-do list
often lacks: it tells you what a competing action is competing with.

## Look below the visible habit

The action you can see is usually the least interesting part of a distraction. Opening
the news, checking messages, or polishing a small task may be the surface response to
boredom, self-doubt, fatigue, loneliness, or the resistance of beginning. The book calls
these inner prompts internal triggers. They do not make an unwanted action inevitable,
but they explain why deleting one app rarely solves the whole problem.

The practical skill is a short pause with an honest label. Instead of saying, "I have no
discipline," ask what sensation arrived just before the urge. Describe it plainly, stay
with it for a moment, and give the planned task a smaller next move. Curiosity turns a
vague impulse into something you can answer. Self-contempt usually adds another feeling
that needs escape.

## Put values on the calendar

An intention has much more leverage when it has an address in time. Rather than treating
the calendar as a record of meetings and the task list as a record of everything else,
the book recommends reserving blocks for the work, relationships, maintenance, and rest
that express your values. A block is a promise about a portion of time, not a prediction
that every hour will go perfectly.

This also makes room for the things people wrongly call distractions by default. Reading
for fun, a show, social media, or a walk can all belong if they have a chosen place. What
matters is that the day is not left as unclaimed space for the loudest request to occupy.
Review the plan after a week, then revise its shape to match actual constraints and the
life you are trying to live.

## Make external cues earn their place

External triggers are the environmental prompts that announce themselves: alerts,
badges, open tabs, meeting requests, a coworker at the desk, or a device within reach.
Some are useful. A reminder to join a meeting can keep a promise; a message from a child
may deserve an immediate answer. The point is not to erase every cue. It is to decide
which cues serve the plan and to change the ones that merely interrupt it.

Treat this as maintenance, not a once-and-for-all purge. Turn off alerts that have no
timely job. Create a visible signal for a focus block. Batch a routine check at a time
you selected. Ask whether a recurring meeting needs a different format. Each adjustment
returns a small decision to the moment before attention is captured.

## Let a prior promise protect a later self

There are moments when noticing an urge and cleaning up the environment are not enough.
That is where a pact belongs. It is a decision made while you are clear-headed that changes
what will be easy, available, or costly later. Leaving the phone outside the room, using a
site blocker during a planned session, or asking a friend to hold you to a commitment all
move the choice upstream.

The book describes several kinds of pact. An effort pact adds a little friction. A price
pact attaches a real cost to abandoning the plan. An identity pact frames follow-through
as the kind of promise you keep. Use the smallest one that helps. A pact should support a
choice you actually endorse, not turn ordinary rest into a rule violation.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Track one recurring detour">
For three days, write down the moment you notice one repeated distraction. Record what you
were about to do, the feeling or situation just before it, and what you did instead. Do not
try to solve the pattern while recording it. Look for the trigger after you have a few
entries.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Give one value a block">
Choose one value that keeps receiving leftover time, such as being a dependable teammate,
moving your body, learning, or calling family. Reserve one realistic block for it this
week. Write the first action inside the block, then treat the result as information for
next week's version.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Audit one source of interruption">
Pick one notification stream, recurring meeting, or open-tab habit. Decide what it is for
and when it deserves an answer. Remove, defer, batch, or redesign the part that has no
job. Keep a useful cue if it serves a commitment you made.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Add one proportionate pact">
For a block that repeatedly slips away, add the least severe barrier that creates a pause:
leave the device outside the room, schedule a blocker, or tell a collaborator when you
will be unavailable. Review after a week. The pact is successful if it protects a choice,
not if it makes the day punitive.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
The claim that distraction starts inside you is a useful place to investigate, not a
verdict that every lapse is personal failure. A noisy home, an understaffed workplace,
caregiving, illness, and inaccessible systems can make focused time genuinely scarce.
Attention conditions also vary widely, including for people with ADHD. Use calendars,
environment changes, and pacts as experiments within the control you have. They are not a
diagnosis, a treatment plan, or a substitute for changing an unreasonable environment.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

Do not wait for a perfectly disciplined moment. Decide what deserves the next block of
time, notice the urge to leave it without contempt, and make the unwanted detour less
available before it arrives.
