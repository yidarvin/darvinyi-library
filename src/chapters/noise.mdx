## The thesis

Kahneman, Sibony, and Sunstein ask us to look beyond the average error in a judgment
system. A hiring panel, court, claims operation, or forecasting team can have no obvious
collective lean and still treat comparable cases very differently. That unwanted spread is
noise. It makes outcomes depend partly on which person happens to decide, what features of
the case catch their attention, and even the conditions of the moment. Fairer, more reliable
judgment starts by measuring the spread, then redesigning the process that permits it.

## Why it matters

Bias is visible because it points somewhere. A group that consistently overvalues a factor
can be shown its average drift. Noise is quieter. A high estimate and a low estimate can
cancel in the average, making a system look centered while individuals receive very different
treatment. That is a serious problem when a judgment determines a diagnosis, a sentence, an
insurance price, a performance rating, or an opportunity to be hired. Consistency is not the
whole of justice or accuracy, but arbitrary variation is not a harmless feature of expertise.

## Bias and noise are different errors

Bias is a systematic miss. If a forecasting process repeatedly predicts too much growth, its
average answer is tilted upward. Noise is the extra scatter around whatever average the system
produces. Two assessors can be equally well-intentioned, receive the same file, and still land
far apart. A system may have bias, noise, both, or neither. Treating every error as bias means
the repair will often chase a directional problem while leaving the lottery intact.

## The average can hide a lottery

Leaders often inspect a portfolio-level result: the average rating, the aggregate payout, the
mean forecast error. Those summaries matter, but they do not answer the question a person
inside the system experiences: would another qualified evaluator have reached a similar
conclusion? If one severe judgment is balanced by one lenient judgment, the average can look
fine while both people have been treated inconsistently. The relevant unit is not only the
score on the dashboard. It is the distribution of judgments for comparable work.

## Variation has more than one source

Some professionals are consistently tougher or more optimistic than their peers. That is one
form of difference. More elusive is the pattern each person carries: one interviewer may prize
polish, another initiative, and a third a familiar background. The same person can also shift
with fatigue, a recent case, a noisy room, or a bad morning. These sources can overlap. The
important practical point is that a shared title and a common rubric do not guarantee a shared
judgment policy.

## Measure the spread before prescribing the cure

Noise cannot be inferred from a few memorable disagreements. A noise audit gives several
qualified people the same representative cases, asks for independent judgments, and examines
how widely the results differ. The exercise is diagnostic, not a public ranking of who has the
best instincts. It can reveal where disagreement concentrates, whether criteria are being
interpreted differently, and whether the stakes justify a more structured method. A single
small audit is a starting sample, not a permanent verdict on a person or profession.

## Good process reduces avoidable variation

The book's response is decision hygiene: safeguards put in place before anyone knows whose
judgment will drift, or which case will provoke it. Break a broad evaluation into separate
questions. Set criteria before reviewing a candidate or proposal. Gather assessments
independently before the meeting creates an anchor. Combine multiple judgments where the task
allows it. Use a simple rule or model when it has proved reliable, while keeping a person
responsible for the cases where context genuinely matters. These moves do not remove judgment.
They give it less room to turn incidental conditions into outcomes.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Name the recurring judgment">
Choose one decision that your team makes often and that matters to the people affected by it:
hiring, prioritization, ratings, estimates, or approvals. State what two genuinely comparable
cases should receive comparable treatment. This defines the system worth inspecting.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Run a small blind comparison">
Select a handful of past or constructed cases, remove identifying details where practical, and
ask several qualified people to assess them privately using the current process. Compare the
range before discussing why it appeared. The goal is to learn about the system, not to win an
argument about the correct answer.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Separate the judgment into parts">
Replace one global verdict with a few observable questions. For a hiring decision, rate the
role-relevant evidence, the quality of examples, and the requirements separately before any
overall recommendation. Agree on the criteria and their meaning before reviewing candidates.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="Combine before you convene">
Have evaluators record their assessment and reasons before a group meeting. Then compare the
independent views, discuss meaningful disagreements, and record why the final decision differs
from the initial range. Independence does not end collaboration. It protects what collaboration
gets to examine.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
Reducing noise is not the same as demanding uniformity. Two cases that differ in relevant ways
should not be forced to the same result, and a narrow scoring rule can hide values that deserve
deliberation. Agreement also does not prove correctness. A whole team can share the same blind
spot, which is bias, while agreeing perfectly. Decision hygiene earns its cost in recurring,
high-stakes judgments where comparable treatment matters. It should be tested for its effects,
not treated as a ritual or an excuse to remove human accountability.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

When two people should reach similar answers but do not, do not assume the disagreement is
just personality. Measure the variation, then give the judgment a process sturdy enough that
the outcome depends more on the case than on the decider or the day.
