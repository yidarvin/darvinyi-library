## The thesis

*Make It Stick* asks learners to distrust a familiar feeling: smooth study is not reliable
evidence of durable learning. Re-reading, highlighting, and repeating one kind of problem
can make material feel close at hand because it is still in view. The more durable route asks
you to retrieve without looking, revisit after time has passed, and mix related problems so
you must decide which idea applies. That work feels less fluent because it makes memory do
the work it will need to do later.

## Why it matters

Most studying is arranged around getting through material. The test, meeting, case, or real
conversation arrives later and asks a different question: can you recognize the cue, rebuild
the idea, and use it without the page in front of you? A learning system should therefore
practice the later demand, not merely repeat the earlier exposure. This matters outside a
classroom too. A new language, a clinical procedure, a sales explanation, or a technical
concept becomes useful only when it can be recalled and selected at the moment it is needed.

## Key ideas

## Familiarity can impersonate knowledge

Seeing an answer again makes it easier to process, and that ease is easy to mistake for
mastery. Recognition answers a generous question: does this look known now? Recall asks the
harder question: can you produce the idea with no prompt beyond the problem? The first is
useful for getting oriented. The second reveals whether a memory can survive outside the
study session. A blank page, a closed-book explanation, a practice problem, or a low-stakes
quiz turns that difference into information. Getting stuck is not proof that the session has
failed. It is a precise signal about what deserves another pass.

## Retrieval is practice, not only a measurement

Tests are commonly treated as an audit after learning has finished. The book treats them as
part of learning itself. Trying to recall a definition, sketching a process from memory, or
solving a problem before consulting the solution strengthens the route back to the knowledge
and exposes the missing pieces. Feedback completes the loop. Check the answer, identify the
specific error, and retrieve again later. The goal is not to manufacture pressure or grade
yourself harshly. It is to create many small occasions when knowledge has to travel from
memory into use.

## Forgetting creates the room that spacing uses

Cramming can raise performance while the material is still warm, but it gives little practice
finding the knowledge after it has faded. Spacing returns to a topic after an interval, when
recall has become effortful but remains possible. Each return asks the learner to reconstruct
the path rather than walk one that is already lit. The appropriate interval is not a magic
number. It depends on how well the material is known and how long it must last. The practical
rule is simple: schedule several brief returns, and let a difficult retrieval pull the next
review closer rather than abandoning the topic.

## Mixing problems trains selection

Blocked practice repeats one problem type until the method becomes obvious. It is efficient
when you are first learning a procedure, but it can quietly supply the answer to a more
important question: which procedure belongs here? Interleaving places neighboring problem
types side by side. The learner has to inspect the features of each problem, distinguish what
changes, and choose a method before carrying it out. That choice makes practice slower and
often less satisfying. It also better resembles a real exam, a diagnosis, or any setting
where the task does not announce its own category.

## Elaboration gives a fact handles

A fact held alone has few routes back. Elaboration adds handles by connecting it to a reason,
an example, a contrast, a picture, or a prior idea. Ask why a claim is true, how it differs
from a tempting alternative, and when it would matter. Explain it in plain language to an
imagined newcomer, then test whether the explanation still works when the familiar notes are
gone. The point is not decorative detail. Good elaboration makes the structure of an idea
more visible, so a later cue has more than one way to find it.

## The Model

The three central moves work as a system. Retrieval makes you reconstruct the knowledge.
Spacing ensures that reconstruction happens after a little forgetting. Interleaving makes
you decide which knowledge belongs to the cue in front of you. None replaces initial
instruction, worked examples, or feedback. They change what happens after the first encounter:
instead of keeping a topic familiar through repeated exposure, you build the ability to call
it back and choose it under changing conditions.

## Put it to work

<ExerciseCard n="01" title="Close the source before you review it">
After reading a section or watching a lesson, close it. Write three things you can recall,
then explain the main relationship in your own words. Open the source only after the attempt.
Correct the gaps with a different color or a short note, so tomorrow's question is visible.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="02" title="Build returns into the calendar">
Choose one topic you want to retain. Give it a ten-minute recall session tomorrow, another
later this week, and one next week. Begin each session with questions or problems, not a
re-read. If the first prompt is impossible, briefly restudy the missing piece and leave a
new question for the next return.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="03" title="Create a mixed set">
Take three related problem types, terms, or cases. Shuffle them into one short session. Before
solving each one, state what kind it is and name the clue that made you choose that approach.
This turns the answer into a decision you can inspect.
</ExerciseCard>

<ExerciseCard n="04" title="End with one better question">
When you check an answer, do not merely mark it right or wrong. Write the next question that
would catch the same misunderstanding from a different angle. Your review system gets stronger
when its prompts evolve with the errors it finds.
</ExerciseCard>

## Where people get it wrong

<Callout kind="note">
Difficulty helps only when it is productive. A learner still needs an initial model, feedback
on errors, and enough support to make a correction possible. Retrieval cannot replace teaching
an unfamiliar idea, and a pile of mixed, unrelated tasks can be noise rather than
interleaving. Spacing schedules returns; it does not excuse avoiding a topic until it is
unrecoverable. The research supports these tools as strong general strategies, not a uniform
recipe for every skill, learner, deadline, or high-stakes setting.
</Callout>

## If you remember one thing

Do not measure a study session by how familiar the page feels. Measure it by whether you can
retrieve the idea later, tell it apart from its neighbors, and use the gaps you find to plan
the next return.
