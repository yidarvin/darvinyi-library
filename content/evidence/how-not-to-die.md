# How Not to Die — research notes

Verified 2026-07-28. These sources support the chapter's original account of a whole-food
plant-centered dietary pattern, the limits of the evidence, and nutritional planning. The chapter
distills ideas from the book and research in original prose; it does not reproduce the book's text
or figures.

- Macmillan, [How Not to Die](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250066114/hownottodie/). The publisher's direct page identifies Michael Greger and Gene Stone's 2015 book and provides the qualifying outbound purchase link. It supports the book identification and its broad preventive dietary thesis, not the chapter's clinical claims.
- Hyunju Kim and colleagues, [Plant-Based Diets Are Associated With a Lower Risk of Incident Cardiovascular Disease, Cardiovascular Disease Mortality, and All-Cause Mortality in a General Population of Middle-Aged Adults](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31387433/) (*Journal of the American Heart Association*, 2019). This ARIC cohort analysis followed 12,168 middle-aged adults from 1987 to 2016. Greater adherence to its plant-based indices was associated with lower incident cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality. The chapter treats these as adjusted observational associations, not proof that diet alone caused the outcomes.
- Jihye Kim and colleagues, [Plant-based dietary patterns and mortality from all causes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer: The Multiethnic Cohort Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38703511/) (*Clinical Nutrition*, 2024). In 144,729 participants followed for an average of 21 years, healthful plant-food scores were associated with lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. The study separately measured less-healthful plant-food scores, supporting the chapter's insistence that a plant label alone does not settle food quality.
- Caroline A. Koch, Emilie W. Kjeldsen, and Ruth Frikke-Schmidt, [Vegetarian or vegan diets and blood lipids: a meta-analysis of randomized trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37226630/) (*European Heart Journal*, 2023). Across 30 randomized trials, vegetarian and vegan interventions lowered total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and apolipoprotein B compared with omnivorous diets. It grounds the cautious statement that randomized evidence supports some risk-factor changes, without establishing long-term disease prevention for every person.
- Angela D. Liese and colleagues, [Effect of a Plant-Based vs Omnivorous Soul Food Diet on Weight and Lipid Levels Among African American Adults](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36633848/) (*JAMA Network Open*, 2023). This two-year randomized clinical trial found no between-group differences in weight or lipid outcomes at twelve months. It is included directly to keep the chapter's caveat concrete: results can vary by intervention, comparison, setting, and adherence.
- National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements, [Vitamin B12 Fact Sheet for Consumers](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/vitaminb12-Consumer/). The fact sheet says plant foods do not naturally supply vitamin B12 unless fortified and flags people who eat little or no animal food as a group who may not get enough. It supports the practice prompt to use a reliable B12 source, without prescribing a dose.
- Dimitra Rafailia Bakaloudi and colleagues, [Intake and adequacy of the vegan diet: A systematic review of the evidence](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33341313/) (*Clinical Nutrition*, 2021). Across 48 included studies, the review found that vegan diets can have low intake of several micronutrients, including B12, vitamin D, iodine, zinc, calcium, and selenium. It supports the page's planning caveat and does not imply that every vegan diet is inadequate.
