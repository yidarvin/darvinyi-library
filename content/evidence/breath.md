# Breath — research notes

Verified 2026-07-27. These primary studies and the publisher record support the chapter's
original account of nasal air conditioning, breathing route, paced breathing, and the limits of
route-based advice. They do not turn a breathing exercise into a treatment claim. The chapter is
an original synthesis of the book's ideas and source material, not a reproduction of its text or
figures.

- Rouadi, Baroody, Abbott, Naureckas, Solway, and Naclerio, [A technique to measure the ability of the human nose to warm and humidify air](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10409601/) (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 1999). In 33 participants, the investigators measured temperature and humidity of air leaving the nasal cavity across controlled airflow challenges. Relative humidity in the nasopharynx remained at 100 percent, while cold, dry airflow made conditioning more demanding. This grounds Key Idea 1 without implying that nasal breathing overcomes obstruction.
- Yasuda, Itoh, Miyamura, and Nishino, [Comparison of exhaled nitric oxide and cardiorespiratory indices between nasal and oral breathing during submaximal exercise in humans](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9504133/) (*Japanese Journal of Physiology*, 1997). Eight healthy participants performed two cycling intensities by each route. Exhaled nitric-oxide output was higher during nasal breathing, while the study found no significant route difference in the measured cardiorespiratory variables. This supports the contextual treatment of Key Idea 2 and guards against a stronger performance claim.
- Joseph, Porta, Casucci, and colleagues, [Slow breathing improves arterial baroreflex sensitivity and decreases blood pressure in essential hypertension](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16129818/) (*Hypertension*, 2005). Twenty adults with essential hypertension and 26 controls completed seated recordings during spontaneous, six-per-minute, and 15-per-minute breathing. The six-per-minute condition increased measured baroreflex sensitivity, with short-term blood-pressure changes in the hypertensive group. This supports Key Idea 3 as an acute physiological observation, not a general treatment recommendation.
- Meurice, Marc, and Sforza, [Effect of nasal or oral breathing route on upper airway resistance during sleep](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14621092/) (*European Respiratory Journal*, 2003). In a randomized single-blind crossover study of 12 healthy adults, upper-airway resistance during supine stage-two sleep was higher in the oral condition. The small experimental setting informs the discussion of route and sleep without being treated as a universal clinical rule.
- Yang, Vena, and colleagues, [Mouth Closure and Airflow in Patients With Obstructive Sleep Apnea: A Nonrandomized Clinical Trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39361293/) (*JAMA Otolaryngology–Head & Neck Surgery*, 2024). Among 54 analyzed patients with obstructive sleep apnea, mouth closure had heterogeneous effects: airflow worsened in participants with high oral breathing and velopharyngeal obstruction. This is the direct basis for the page's warning against mouth taping as a default.
- Penguin Random House, [Breath by James Nestor](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/600149/breath-by-james-nestor/). Used only for the page's outbound purchase link and publication record.
