# The Innovators — research notes

Verified 2026-07-28. This chapter is an original synthesis of Walter Isaacson's account of
the digital revolution as cumulative, collaborative work. It does not reproduce the book's
prose, sequence, stories, figures, or cover art. Every diagram is an original conceptual SVG
drawn from this library's diagram vocabulary.

- Simon & Schuster, [*The Innovators*](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Innovators/Walter-Isaacson/9781476708706). The publisher page confirms the book, author, and 2014 edition. It also supplies the chapter's outward purchase link.
- Computer History Museum, ["A Brief History"](https://www.computerhistory.org/babbage/history/). The museum's account of Babbage's Analytical Engine and Lovelace's 1843 notes supports the chapter's opening claim that programmable computation was conceived before a complete working general-purpose machine. It also supports the distinction between a machine and instructions for it.
- Computer History Museum, ["1947: Invention of the Point-Contact Transistor"](https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/invention-of-the-point-contact-transistor/). The museum documents Bardeen and Brattain's successful semiconductor amplifier in December 1947, the Bell Labs research group around it, and the device's public announcement. It supports the chapter's account of electronic components as collaborative, material work rather than an isolated breakthrough.
- Computer History Museum, ["The Integrated Circuit"](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/digital-revolution/12/276). This artifact-based history records Kilby's 1958 working circuit and the effort to package growing numbers of electronic components. It informs the chapter's broad claim that shrinking and integrating components made new forms of computing practical, without presenting a quantitative history of cost or scale.
- Computer History Museum, ["Internet History: the 1960s"](https://www.computerhistory.org/internethistory/1960s/). The museum describes the teams at UCLA, SRI, BBN, and other sites, the RFC process, and the first UCLA-to-SRI host connection in 1969. It supports the chapter's treatment of shared protocols and institution-spanning handoffs.
- NASA, ["Margaret Hamilton"](https://science.nasa.gov/people/margaret-hamilton/). NASA identifies Hamilton as leader of the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory's Software Engineering Division for Apollo guidance work and credits her with popularizing software engineering. This supports the chapter's recognition that programming, testing, and operational reliability are substantive contributions, not mere afterthoughts.

### Scope note

The timeline and diagrams are thematic, not a reconstruction of the book's chronology or of any
historical figure. They omit many contributors and episodes by design so the page can explain a
general pattern: durable digital systems grow through handoffs among concepts, materials,
software, standards, maintenance, and use. The caveat explicitly warns against turning that
pattern into a claim that every collaborator had equal power, credit, or responsibility.
