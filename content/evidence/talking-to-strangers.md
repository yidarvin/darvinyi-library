# Talking to Strangers — research notes

Verified 2026-07-27. These primary sources and original research records support the
chapter's account of truth-default, limits on reading internal states from demeanor, and
the role of situational context. The chapter is an original synthesis of the book's ideas
and source material, not a reproduction of its text or figures.

- Malcolm Gladwell, [*Talking to Strangers*](https://www.gladwellbooks.com/titles/malcolm-gladwell/talking-to-strangers/9780316299220/), Back Bay Books. The publisher's direct book page identifies the book, author, and its examination of failed encounters with unfamiliar people. It supplies the page's purchase path.
- Timothy R. Levine, ["Truth-Default Theory (TDT): A Theory of Human Deception and Deception Detection"](https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X14535916), *Journal of Language and Social Psychology*, 2014. Levine's original theoretical article describes truth-default as a tendency to believe others and argues that contextual communication content matters more than a generic catalogue of nonverbal cues. It supports the chapter's matrix distinction between ordinary provisional trust and independent checks prompted by conflicting evidence or consequential decisions.
- Thomas Gilovich, Kenneth Savitsky, and Victoria Husted Medvec, ["The Illusion of Transparency: Biased Assessments of Others' Ability to Read One's Emotional States"](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.75.2.332), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1998. Across three sets of studies, the authors found that participants overestimated how detectable their internal states were to others. This supports the chapter's cautious treatment of visible demeanor as incomplete evidence.
- Yuichi Shoda, Walter Mischel, and Jack C. Wright, ["Intraindividual Stability in the Organization and Patterning of Behavior: Incorporating Psychological Situations into the Idiographic Analysis of Personality"](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.4.674), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1994. The original paper studies patterns of behavior across psychological situations. It supports the narrower claim that conduct should be interpreted with the setting in view, not the stronger claim that situations fully determine a person.
- Timothy R. Levine and Hee Sun Park, ["Accuracy in Detecting Truths and Lies: Documenting the Veracity Effect"](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637759909376468), *Communication Monographs*, 1999. The authors report that, in their data, accuracy for truths exceeded chance while detecting lies was often below chance. This supports refusing to treat confidence in deception detection as a reliable substitute for corroboration.
