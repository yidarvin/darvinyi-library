# Astrophysics for People in a Hurry — research notes

Verified 2026-07-28. These institutional and author sources support the chapter's factual
claims and scope. The chapter's prose and diagrams are original synthesis, not a
reproduction of the book, its figures, or its expression.

- Neil deGrasse Tyson, [Astrophysics For People in a Hurry](https://neildegrassetyson.com/books/2017-05-astrophysics-for-people-in-a-hurry/). The author's book page confirms the 2017 Norton publication and its intended range, from the Big Bang and black holes to planets and the search for life. It is also the chapter's outward purchase link.
- NASA Science, [Overview: The Universe](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/overview/). NASA's current history of the universe supports the early hot expansion, primordial light elements, recombination roughly 380,000 years later, the cosmic microwave background, and the transition to the first stars and galaxies. Figure 129.1 uses a deliberately conceptual timeline, not a scale drawing or a reproduction of a mission image.
- NASA Science, [What Can We Learn from the Universe's Baby Picture?](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stories/quick-reads/what-can-we-learn-from-the-universes-baby-picture/). The agency explains that the cosmic microwave background is the oldest observable light, formed about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, and that its small temperature variations trace seeds of later galaxies. This informs the caveat distinguishing it from an image of the beginning itself.
- NASA Science, [Are we really made of star stuff?](https://science.nasa.gov/astrobiology/learning-resources/alp/are-we-really-made-of-star-stuff/). Supports the distinction between Big Bang nucleosynthesis, stellar fusion up to iron, and heavy-element production in explosive stellar events. Figure 129.3 reduces those processes to an original material-recycling loop and does not claim that all elements share one origin.
- NASA Science, [Dark Matter](https://science.nasa.gov/dark-matter/). Supports the inference of dark matter from visible objects' motion and gravitational lensing, and its role in large-scale organization. Figure 129.4 is an original conceptual system map, not a dark-matter map.
- NASA Science, [Building Blocks](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/overview/building-blocks/). Supports the approximate standard cosmological split of 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter, and 68% dark energy, the accelerating expansion, and the present uncertainty about the natures of the dark components. Figure 129.5 labels the values as approximate and makes no claim that they are directly observed substances.
- European Space Agency, [Planck and the cosmic microwave background](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Planck/Planck_and_the_cosmic_microwave_background). Cross-checks the standard model's use of dark matter as a structure-forming scaffold and dark energy as the component associated with accelerated expansion, while noting their lack of experimental confirmation.
