# A Guide to the Good Life — research notes

Verified 2026-07-28. The publisher and author sources were consulted for the book's scope,
publication details, and practical emphasis; the ancient primary text was consulted for the
Stoic distinction that informs the chapter's account of agency and externals. The chapter is
original synthesis and its diagrams are original conceptual illustrations, not reproductions of
the book or any source figure.

- William B. Irvine, [*A Guide to the Good Life: The Ancient Art of Stoic Joy*](https://academic.oup.com/book/50088). Oxford University Press identifies the 2008 book, describes its project as applying Stoic psychological techniques to dissatisfaction and worry, and lists negative visualization, control, self-denial, meditation, duty, and social relations among its topics. This is the primary publisher record for the book and the chapter's outbound reader link.
- William B. Irvine, [Books](https://www.williambirvine.com/books). The author's own site describes *A Guide to the Good Life* as an accessible, practical introduction to Stoicism. It supports the chapter's framing of the work as a modern program rather than a scholarly reconstruction of every ancient doctrine.
- Epictetus, [*Enchiridion*, sections 1–5 and 10](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html). The ancient primary text distinguishes a person's judgments, pursuits, desires, and actions from body, property, reputation, office, and other externals. It also directs readers to examine a troubling appearance and make fitting use of an accident. It grounds the chapter's caveat that a modern, action-oriented three-ring heuristic is not identical to Epictetus's sharper distinction.
