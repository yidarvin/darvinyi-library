# Outlive — research notes

Verified 2026-07-27. These primary studies, public-health guidance, and the author's official
book page support the chapter's original account of healthspan, prevention, exercise, and the
limits of longevity claims. The chapter is an original synthesis of the book's ideas and source
material, not a reproduction of its text or figures.

- Peter Attia, [Outlive: The Science & Art of Longevity](https://peterattiamd.com/outlive/). The author's official book page describes the project's shift from late treatment toward earlier prevention and identifies heart disease, cancer, Alzheimer's disease, and type 2 diabetes as central diseases of aging. It supports the chapter's account of the book's thesis and four-front grouping, not any clinical recommendation.
- Imboden, Harber, Whaley, Finch, Bishop, and Kaminsky, [Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Mortality in Healthy Men and Women](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30384883/) (*Journal of the American College of Cardiology*, 2018). This prospective cohort followed 4,137 apparently healthy adults after directly measuring cardiorespiratory fitness. Higher baseline fitness was associated with lower all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality. The chapter treats this as an association and does not make fitness a personal forecast or proof of causation.
- World Health Organization, [Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour: recommendations for adults](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK566046/), 2020. The guideline recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity per week, plus muscle strengthening on two or more days. It also states that activity should be increased gradually. This grounds the cautious practice prompt, which does not replace individualized clearance or rehabilitation advice.
- National Institute on Aging, [What Do We Know About Healthy Aging?](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/healthy-aging/what-do-we-know-about-healthy-aging). The NIA identifies activity, food choices, sleep, regular health care, and mental health as controllable contributors to healthy aging, while noting that genetics and other factors also matter. This supports the chapter's wider account of capacity and emotional health.
- Hands, Lustgarten, Frame, and Rosen, [What is the clinical evidence to support off-label rapamycin therapy in healthy adults?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40778880/) (*Aging*, 2025). This review concludes that human data have not established rapamycin or its analogues as proven therapies to delay aging in healthy older adults. It is the direct basis for the caveat against self-prescribing longevity drugs.
