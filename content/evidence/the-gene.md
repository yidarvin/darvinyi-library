# The Gene — research notes

Verified 2026-07-26. These primary institutional sources support the chapter's account of
genes, gene expression, the history of genomic sequencing, genome editing, and its ethical
caveats. The chapter is original synthesis, not a reproduction of the book or its images.

- National Human Genome Research Institute, [Gene](https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Gene). The institute defines genes as basic units of inheritance and explains that a broader definition includes DNA segments that make functional RNA, not only protein-coding sequences. This informs the chapter's account of the gene as a working biological concept rather than a single kind of command.
- National Human Genome Research Institute, [DNA fact sheet](https://www.genome.gov/about-genomics/fact-sheets/Deoxyribonucleic-Acid-Fact-Sheet). This source supports the chapter's statements that DNA stores biological instructions, is packaged into chromosomes, and includes regulatory sequence outside protein-coding regions.
- National Human Genome Research Institute, [Gene expression](https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Gene-Expression) and [RNA fact sheet](https://www.genome.gov/about-genomics/educational-resources/fact-sheets/ribonucleic-acid-fact-sheet). These sources support Figure 84.2 and the explanation that expression converts genomic information into RNA products, with regulation varying across conditions and cell types.
- National Human Genome Research Institute, [Genetic timeline](https://www.genome.gov/Pages/Education/GeneticTimeline.pdf). The timeline records the rediscovery of Mendel's work, chromosome theory, and the coinage of the term “gene.” Figure 84.1 is an original conceptual timeline, not a reconstruction of this source.
- National Institutes of Health, [First complete sequence of a human genome](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/nih-research-matters/first-complete-sequence-human-genome). The agency reports that the Human Genome Project's 2003 reference sequence covered about 92% of the genome and that later work completed a gap-free human genome sequence. This supports the chapter's distinction between genome reading as an evolving capability and a final biological answer.
- National Human Genome Research Institute, [Genetic testing FAQ](https://www.genome.gov/FAQ/Genetic-Testing). This source supports the chapter's cautions that testing cannot answer everything about inherited disease, a positive result does not always predict disease, and GINA has limits for long-term care, disability, and life insurance.
- National Human Genome Research Institute, [Eugenics and Scientific Racism](https://www.genome.gov/about-genomics/fact-sheets/Eugenics-and-Scientific-Racism). The institute documents eugenics as inaccurate, discriminatory, and built on a prejudiced misuse of Mendelian genetics. This grounds the chapter's historical and ethical warning.
- U.S. Food and Drug Administration, [CASGEVY](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/casgevy) and [2023 summary basis for regulatory action](https://www.fda.gov/media/175179/download). These records describe an approved ex vivo CRISPR/Cas9 treatment that edits a patient's own hematopoietic stem cells and requires conditioning before infusion. They inform the chapter's contrast between concrete therapeutic intervention and a simple technological slogan.
- World Health Organization, [Current capabilities for human genome editing](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-SCI-RFH-2021.06-eng). The expert advisory report says environmental and other variables can make exact outcomes difficult to predict in both somatic and heritable applications. This supports the chapter's discussion of uncertainty and governance.
- Bookshop.org, [The Gene: An Intimate History](https://bookshop.org/p/books/the-gene-an-intimate-history-siddhartha-mukherjee/12241880). Used only for the page's outbound purchase link.
