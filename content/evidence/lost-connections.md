# Lost Connections — research notes

Verified 2026-07-28. These sources inform this chapter's original synthesis of
*Lost Connections*. The prose and conceptual SVGs are new work. They do not reproduce
the book's text, figures, or cover.

- Johann Hari, [*Lost Connections*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/lost-connections-9781526634085/), Bloomsbury. The publisher describes the book's central challenge to a solely biological account of depression and anxiety, and confirms the author and edition used for this page.
- Alan R. Teo et al., ["Social relationships and depression: ten-year follow-up from a nationally representative study"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23646128/), *PLOS ONE*, 2013. In a cohort of 4,642 U.S. adults, poorer baseline relationship quality was associated with higher risk of a later major depressive episode. The study also found that social isolation, measured by partner status and contact frequency, did not predict future depression, a useful warning against reducing connection to headcount.
- M. K. Wray et al., ["Trans-ancestry genome-wide study of depression identifies 697 associations implicating cell types and pharmacotherapies"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39814019/), *Cell*, 2025. This large genome-wide association study identifies many genetic associations with major depression and supports the chapter's caution that social explanations do not displace biological ones.
- Senay Asfaw et al., ["Precarious Work as Risk Factor for 5-Year Increase in Depressive Symptoms"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35328860/), *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 2022. A German employee cohort associated precarious work conditions with subsequent depressive symptoms. It supports treating work conditions as a health-relevant context, not proof that work alone causes depression.
- Eliza K. Rogerson et al., ["A randomized controlled trial of surf and hike therapy for U.S. active duty service members with major depressive disorder"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36805672/), *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 2023. Both outdoor group interventions were adjunctive treatments in this particular clinical sample, not stand-alone cures. This informs the chapter's restrained discussion of nature and activity.

### Scope note

This chapter is an account of a social view of depression and anxiety, not medical advice or a
replacement for assessment and treatment. Social conditions can matter deeply while medication,
psychotherapy, sleep, physical illness, trauma, and inherited vulnerability may also matter. Do not
start, stop, or change treatment from this page. For immediate risk of self-harm, contact local
emergency services or a crisis service.
