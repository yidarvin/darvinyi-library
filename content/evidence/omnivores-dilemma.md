# The Omnivore's Dilemma — research notes

Verified 2026-07-28. These sources support the chapter's original account of food chains, the
many roles of U.S. corn, and the boundaries of organic certification. The chapter distills ideas
from the book and current source material in original prose; it does not reproduce the book's text
or figures.

- Michael Pollan, [The Omnivore's Dilemma](https://michaelpollan.com/books/the-omnivores-dilemma/). The author's official book page describes the book's inquiry into industrial, organic or alternative, and self-provisioned food chains. It supports the chapter's account of the book's organizing question and its broad thesis.
- U.S. Department of Agriculture Economic Research Service, [Feed Grains Sector at a Glance](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/crops/corn-and-other-feed-grains/feed-grains-sector-at-a-glance). The USDA identifies corn as the primary U.S. feed grain and describes its major domestic uses in livestock feed, ethanol, food ingredients, and industrial products. It supports the corn-as-system example, without treating its current figures as claims about the book's 2006 conditions.
- U.S. Department of Agriculture Agricultural Marketing Service, [Organic Livestock & Dairy](https://www.ams.usda.gov/grades-standards/organic-livestock-dairy). The National Organic Program's overview states that organic livestock requires organic feed and forage, prohibits antibiotics and hormones, and requires access to pasture for ruminants. It supports the chapter's bounded description of organic livestock standards.
- U.S. Department of Agriculture Agricultural Marketing Service, [The National List](https://www.ams.usda.gov/rules-regulations/organic/national-list). This primary regulatory source explains the allowed-and-prohibited-substances framework for organic crop, livestock, and handling production. It grounds the distinction between a certification's rules and broader judgments about a food system.
- U.S. Department of Agriculture Economic Research Service, [ERS Food Dollar's Three Series Show Distributions of U.S. Food Production Costs](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2023/december/ers-food-dollar-s-three-series-show-distributions-of-u-s-food-production-costs). ERS separates a consumer food dollar into farm and marketing shares, and explains that processing, transport, retail, food service, and other activities sit between farm commodities and food purchases. It supports the page's claim that a checkout price does not describe every condition or cost in a supply chain; it does not support a claim that any particular food is underpriced.
