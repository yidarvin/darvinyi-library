# A Mind for Numbers — research notes

Verified 2026-07-28. These publisher and research sources inform the chapter's account of
Barbara Oakley's study framework and its evidence base. The chapter is original synthesis, and
its diagrams are original conceptual illustrations rather than copies of a book or research
figure.

- Barbara Oakley, [*A Mind for Numbers*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/314056/a-mind-for-numbers-by-barbara-oakley-phd/), Penguin Random House (2014). The publisher identifies the book as Oakley's companion to *Learning How to Learn* and provides the reader link used on the page.
- Roediger and Karpicke, [Test-Enhanced Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x), *Psychological Science* (2006). In experiments using prose passages, the authors compared repeated studying with repeated testing and found that retrieval practice improved later retention. It supports the chapter's advice to close the page and attempt recall before checking.
- Cepeda et al., [Spacing Effects in Learning](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x/), *Psychological Science* (2008). Across more than 1,350 participants learning facts, the study varied the gap before a review and the delay before a final test. It informs the chapter's recommendation to return later, and its caveat that timing depends on the intended retention interval.
- Rohrer and Dedrick, [Interleaved Practice Improves Mathematics Learning](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1071568), *Journal of Educational Psychology* (2015). This classroom study compared mixed and blocked mathematics practice. It informs the chapter's distinction between initial repetition for a new technique and later practice that requires choosing a method.
- Sio and Ormerod, [Does Incubation Enhance Problem Solving?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19210055/), *Psychological Bulletin* (2009). This meta-analysis found incubation effects that varied with problem type and the intervening activity. It grounds the page's restrained claim that a break can sometimes change a search, not that rest reliably solves problems by itself.

### Scope note

The focused/diffuse distinction is presented here as a practical study heuristic. It should not
be read as a claim that cognition divides into two discrete neural states or that every break
creates insight. The research above supports specific learning practices under particular
conditions; it does not establish a universal schedule, a fixed learning style, or a guarantee
of transfer across every subject.
