# When Breath Becomes Air — research notes

Verified 2026-07-28. These sources support the chapter's original account of Kalanithi's
professional context, the memoir's central question, and the care principle that medical decisions
in serious illness should account for a person's priorities. The chapter distills ideas from the
book and source record in original prose; it does not reproduce the book's text or figures.

- Penguin Random House, [When Breath Becomes Air](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/258507/when-breath-becomes-air-by-paul-kalanithi/). The publisher's primary product page identifies the 2016 posthumous memoir, its author, and its central transition from Stanford neurosurgeon to patient with stage IV lung cancer. It supports the book identification, broad scope, and outbound purchase link.
- Stanford Medicine, [Paul Kalanithi, writer and neurosurgeon, dies at 37](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2015/03/stanford-neurosurgeon-writer-paul-kalanithi-dies-at-37.html). Stanford's contemporaneous institutional obituary documents Kalanithi's training, neurosurgery residency, 2013 diagnosis, return to work, and death in 2015. It supports the clinical timeline used as context, not a claim that every person with cancer has the same course.
- Stanford Medicine, [The Kalanithi legacy: “Paul wanted his life to have meaning”](https://med.stanford.edu/news/insights/2025/07/paul-kalanithi-book-legacy-when-breath-becomes-air.html). This interview with Lucy Kalanithi describes the manuscript's completion, posthumous publication, and the book's concern with witnessing suffering and remaining present to patients. It informs the chapter's account of writing, care, and legacy without supplying its language.
- Susan D. Block and colleagues, [Communication about serious illness care goals: a review and synthesis of best practices](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25330167/) (*JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2014). This evidence review concludes that understanding a seriously ill patient's goals helps align care with what matters to that person. It supports the chapter's practical prompt to discuss priorities and tradeoffs with clinicians, not any particular treatment decision.
- Lisa Rosenbaum, [Falling Together: Empathetic Care for the Dying](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJMms1516444) (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 2016). This contemporary essay considers the questions Kalanithi's memoir raises about living while dying, physician support, empathy, and hope. It is included as a clinical reading of the book's subject, not as evidence for a universal experience of dying.
