# The Total Money Makeover — research notes

Verified 2026-07-27. These first-party and primary public sources support this chapter's
original account of Ramsey's ordered debt-payoff framework. The chapter is a synthesis in new
prose and original diagrams. It does not reproduce the book's text, worksheets, case studies,
or figures.

- Dave Ramsey, [*The Total Money Makeover Updated and Expanded*](https://www.thomasnelson.com/9781400342525/the-total-money-makeover-updated-and-expanded/), Thomas Nelson. The publisher identifies Ramsey as author and describes the book as a personal-finance program organized around saving habits, debt reduction, and building wealth. This page is also the chapter's outbound purchase link.
- Ramsey Solutions, [*What are the 7 Baby Steps?*](https://help.ramseysolutions.com/hc/en-us/articles/360046646012-What-are-the-7-Baby-Steps). Ramsey's official help center gives the current seven-stage order: starter emergency savings; non-mortgage debt repayment with the debt snowball; a fully funded emergency fund; retirement investment; college savings; early mortgage payoff; then wealth building and giving. This supports the chapter's account of the plan as a sequence rather than a set of simultaneous targets.
- Ramsey Solutions, [*How the Debt Snowball Method Works*](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/how-the-debt-snowball-method-works). The official guide describes listing debts from smallest balance to largest, paying required amounts on the others, directing extra money to the smallest balance, and applying the released payment to the next balance. It is the direct source for the debt-snowball mechanism, while the chapter's diagram is an independent visual explanation.
- Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, [*How to reduce your debt*](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/blog/how-reduce-your-debt/). The CFPB contrasts the highest-interest-rate method with the snowball method. It says the former can save money in the long run, while the latter can create quick visible progress but may cost more. This supports the caveat and the chapter's distinction between behavioral momentum and interest minimization.
- Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, [*Starting Small Can Lead to Big Savings*](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-resource-center/2024-01/starting-small-can-lead-big-savings). The FDIC discusses separate emergency savings and says a goal of at least six months of living expenses can help cover a job loss, car repair, or unexpected medical expense. This supports the chapter's distinction between a starter buffer and a larger reserve, without treating either amount as universal advice.
- Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, [student-loan debt tips](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/paying-for-college/repay-student-debt/student-loan-debt-tips/). The CFPB notes that borrower-specific repayment options and the handling of extra payments can matter, particularly for federal student loans. This informs the caveat against applying a generic sequence without checking current loan protections and repayment terms.

The chapter deliberately treats the plan as an approach to behavior and cash flow, not as
individualized financial, tax, legal, credit, or debt advice. Its caveat avoids presenting the
snowball as the universally cheapest repayment method or a fixed emergency fund as adequate
for every household.
