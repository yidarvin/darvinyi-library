# Stumbling on Happiness — research notes

Verified 2026-07-27. These primary sources and the author's research record support the
chapter's original account of affective forecasting, focalism, immune neglect, and
surrogation. The chapter is an original synthesis of the book's ideas and source material,
not a reproduction of its text or figures.

- Daniel Gilbert, [*Stumbling on Happiness*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/59967/stumbling-on-happiness-by-daniel-gilbert/). The publisher's book page identifies the 2006 ebook edition and provides the chapter's outbound purchase path. Its description frames the book around imagination, foresight, and mistaken expectations of satisfaction.
- Timothy D. Wilson and Daniel T. Gilbert, [Affective Forecasting](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Wilson%20%26%20Gilbert%20%28Advances%29.pdf), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 2003. This primary review distinguishes forecasts of valence, specific emotion, intensity, and duration. It supports the chapter's caveat that directional predictions can be useful even where the scale and persistence of a reaction are misestimated.
- Timothy D. Wilson, Thalia Wheatley, Jonathan Meyers, Daniel Gilbert, and Danny Axsom, [Focalism: A Source of Durability Bias in Affective Forecasting](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Wilson%20et%20al%20%28Focalism%29.pdf), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2000. The experiments support the chapter's explanation that forecasters underweight unrelated events that will later shape attention and feeling.
- Daniel T. Gilbert, Elizabeth Pinel, Timothy D. Wilson, Stephen Blumberg, and Thalia Wheatley, [Immune Neglect: A Source of Durability Bias in Affective Forecasting](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Gilbert%20et%20al%20%28IMMUNE%20NEGLECT%29.pdf), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1998. Across six studies, the authors examine how people overlook their own sense-making and coping processes when predicting reactions to negative events. This supports the chapter's limited account of immune neglect, including its refusal to treat coping as guaranteed or morally required.
- Daniel T. Gilbert, Matthew A. Killingsworth, Rebecca N. Eyre, and Timothy D. Wilson, [The Surprising Power of Neighborly Advice](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/GILBERT%20KILLINGSWORTH%20ET%20AL%20%282009%29.pdf), *Science*, 2009. In two experiments, participants made more accurate forecasts from another person's affective report than from event information, while often believing the opposite. This supports the chapter's practical use of comparable lived reports as one input, not as a substitute for individual circumstances.
