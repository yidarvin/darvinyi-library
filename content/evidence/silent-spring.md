# Silent Spring — research notes

Verified 2026-07-28. These sources inform this chapter's original synthesis of
*Silent Spring*. The prose and conceptual SVGs are new work. They do not reproduce the
book's text, figures, or cover.

- Rachel Carson, [*Silent Spring*](https://bookshop.org/p/books/silent-spring-rachel-carson/17888229), Houghton Mifflin, 1962. The primary work concerns the consequences of widespread pesticide use; this chapter organizes its ideas thematically rather than following its sequence or wording.
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, ["DDT: A Brief History and Status"](https://www.epa.gov/ingredients-used-pesticide-products/ddt-brief-history-and-status). EPA describes DDT as persistent, able to accumulate in fatty tissue, and able to travel long distances. It also records the 1972 cancellation order and the book's role in public concern about pesticide controls.
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, ["DDT Regulatory History: A Brief Survey"](https://www.epa.gov/archive/epa/aboutepa/ddt-regulatory-history-brief-survey-1975.html), 1975. This agency history records early warnings, broad postwar use, growing insect resistance, and the regulatory debate. It supports the chapter's historical account without treating one book as the sole cause of later policy.
- U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, [*Northern States Bald Eagle Recovery Plan*](https://ecos.fws.gov/docs/recovery_plan/060309b.pdf), 2003. The recovery plan describes DDE, a DDT breakdown product, as interfering with reproduction through eggshell thinning, particularly in birds near the end of long food chains. It informs the food-web explanation and the caution that effects depend on species and exposure.
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, ["Integrated Pest Management Principles"](https://www.epa.gov/safepestcontrol/integrated-pest-management-ipm-principles). EPA defines a four-part approach: set action thresholds, monitor and identify, prevent, and control. It says broadcast spraying of non-specific pesticides is a last resort. This is later practice, not a framework claimed as Carson's own.
- U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, ["Slowing and Combating Pest Resistance to Pesticides"](https://www.epa.gov/pesticide-registration/slowing-and-combating-pest-resistance-pesticides). EPA says pesticide resistance can reduce field performance over time and recommends IPM strategies to reduce selection pressure. It supports the chapter's resistance-loop diagram.
- Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, ["Indoor Residual Spraying Prevention Strategies"](https://www.cdc.gov/malaria/php/public-health-strategy/irs-strategies.html), 2024. CDC describes targeted indoor residual spraying as one malaria-prevention method. It supports the caveat that pesticide discussion must not erase disease-control benefits.

### Scope note

This chapter explains an environmental argument and a way to ask better questions about pest
control. It is not application advice, a product recommendation, or a replacement for local
public-health, agricultural-extension, or regulatory guidance. Pest-control choices depend on
the organism, setting, exposure pathway, and applicable rules.
